> **TL;DR** — Lunar New Year 2027 lands on Saturday, February 6. If you operate a restaurant, bakery, salon, florist, or gift shop anywhere from Monterey Park to Arcadia, this constitutes one of the largest local commercial weeks of the year, and for a good many of these businesses it exceeds Valentine's Day. Begin your Google Business Profile posts and any paid promotion by mid-January rather than during the week itself.

[Lunar New Year 2027 falls on February 6](https://chinesenewyear.net/), beginning the Year of the Goat — the Fire Goat specifically, and also called the Sheep or Ram depending on who is translating. If your business sits in Monterey Park, Alhambra, San Gabriel, Rowland Heights, Arcadia, or San Marino — the SGV cities carrying the largest Chinese American populations, where Lunar New Year constitutes a genuine commercial event rather than a footnote — this is a week you plan for rather than react to.

The commercial window is also considerably longer than most non-observing owners assume. The Spring Festival runs fifteen days and closes with the Lantern Festival on February 20th, so a business that prepares only for the weekend itself has planned for roughly a seventh of the period during which people are actually spending.

One thing deserves stating plainly: "Lunar New Year" is not a single holiday carrying a single set of customs. It constitutes the largest holiday of the year for Chinese communities, where it is frequently called Chinese New Year or Spring Festival. But Vietnamese communities observe Tết and Korean communities observe Seollal around the same lunar calendar, and their foods, colors, and rituals are not identical to Chinese traditions even where they overlap considerably. If your customer base is mixed, which in the SGV it ordinarily is, do not market as though a monolithic "Asian New Year" existed. Know which community you are genuinely addressing, or keep your language general enough that you are not putting Tết vocabulary in a Chinese customer's mouth or the reverse.

## What actually drives foot traffic

A handful of specific customs translate into genuine revenue if you are prepared for them:

**Red envelopes (hongbao in Mandarin, lai see in Cantonese, li xi in Vietnamese).** Cash gifts presented in red paper envelopes, traditionally given by married or older relatives to children and to unmarried adults. This is precisely why banks and check-cashing businesses experience a genuine increase across the fortnight preceding New Year, because people want crisp, unworn bills — handing somebody a wrinkled bill inside a red envelope constitutes a minor social error. If you operate any variety of financial or check-cashing service, "fresh bills available" is a genuinely specific thing to advertise rather than filler copy.

**Reunion dinners.** The evening before New Year is the single largest restaurant night of the season, and families reserve banquet rooms weeks ahead. If you run a restaurant possessing private rooms or a banquet menu, that represents your highest-value booking of the entire year, and it needs to be reservable well before the holiday arrives.

**Gifts of food, rather than merely eating out.** Boxes of mandarin oranges and pomelos, whose round shape and gold color signify wealth, alongside nian gao (sticky rice cake, a pun on "higher year") and gift tins of candy or pastries, all move as presents between family members and business relationships alike. Bakeries and gift shops that assemble a pre-made gift box spare customers a decision and ordinarily sell considerably better than those asking people to construct their own.

**Fresh flowers and plants tied to luck.** Kumquat trees, where the fruit again reads as gold and therefore wealth, alongside narcissus and peach or plum blossoms, constitute the traditional purchases — not roses, and not generic "spring" arrangements. A florist stocking the actual plants people are searching for, and saying so in plain language, comfortably beats one that merely hangs red bunting.

**Looking good for the new year.** Obtaining a haircut before New Year is common practice, because cutting hair during the first several days of the new year is traditionally avoided, so salons experience a rush across the week or two beforehand and then a genuine lull immediately afterward. If you operate a salon, that is worth scheduling additional chairs for, rather than worth additional advertising spend during the dead week.

## Doing it right, not just doing it loud

Red and gold decor in your storefront or across your homepage banner is perfectly acceptable as a signal, but it does not constitute marketing on its own, because every business on the block will have it. What genuinely moves people:

* **Google Business Profile posts written in the customer's language**, rather than English carrying a red border. A Mandarin or Cantonese post describing your reunion-dinner menu, published well ahead of time, appears in local search results for people searching in that language.
* **Local Chinese-language press and community calendars.** World Journal and Sing Tao Daily still carry genuine classified and event listings in this area, and community Facebook groups for Monterey Park, Alhambra, and Arcadia generate real engagement across the weeks preceding the holiday. This is not nostalgia advertising; it is where the audience actually looks.
* **A specific, bookable offer**, rather than a vague sale. "Reunion dinner menu, book by \[date]" comfortably beats "Happy Lunar New Year, everyone welcome."

None of this demands a substantial budget. It demands knowing what the holiday actually means to the people walking into your business, and saying something specific about it rather than hanging a banner and hoping.
