> **TL;DR** — Lunar New Year 2027 lands on Saturday, February 6. If you're a restaurant, bakery, salon, florist, or gift shop anywhere from Monterey Park to Arcadia, this is one of the biggest local commercial weeks of the year — bigger than Valentine's Day for a lot of these businesses. Start your Google Business Profile posts and any paid promotion by mid-January, not the week of.

Lunar New Year 2027 falls on February 6, the start of the Year of the Goat (also called the Sheep or Ram, depending on who's translating). If your business sits in Monterey Park, Alhambra, San Gabriel, Rowland Heights, Arcadia, or San Marino — the cities in the SGV with the largest Chinese American populations, and where Lunar New Year is a real commercial event, not a footnote — this is a week you plan for, not react to.

One thing worth saying plainly: "Lunar New Year" isn't one holiday with one set of customs. It's the biggest holiday of the year for Chinese communities, where it's often called Chinese New Year or Spring Festival. But Vietnamese communities celebrate Tết and Korean communities celebrate Seollal around the same lunar calendar, and their foods, colors, and rituals aren't identical to Chinese traditions even though they overlap. If your customer base is mixed — and in the SGV it usually is — don't market as if there's one monolithic "Asian New Year." Know which community you're actually talking to, or keep your language general enough that you're not putting Tết words in a Chinese customer's mouth or vice versa.

## What actually drives foot traffic

A few specific customs turn into real revenue if you're set up for them:

**Red envelopes (hongbao in Mandarin, lai see in Cantonese, li xi in Vietnamese).** Cash gifts in red paper envelopes, traditionally given by married or older relatives to children and unmarried adults. This is why banks and check-cashing businesses see a real bump in the two weeks before New Year — people want crisp, unworn bills, because handing someone a wrinkled bill in a red envelope is a minor faux pas. If you run any kind of financial or check-cashing service, "fresh bills available" is a real, specific thing to advertise, not filler copy.

**Reunion dinners.** The night before New Year is the single biggest restaurant night of the season — families book out banquet rooms weeks in advance. If you're a restaurant with private rooms or a banquet menu, that's your highest-value booking of the year, and it needs to be reservable well before the holiday.

**Gifts of food, not just eating out.** Boxes of mandarin oranges and pomelos (the round shape and gold color mean wealth), nian gao (sticky rice cake, a pun on "higher year"), and gift tins of candy or pastries move as presents between family and business relationships. Bakeries and gift shops that put together a pre-made gift box save customers a decision and usually sell better than asking people to build their own.

**Fresh flowers and plants tied to luck.** Kumquat trees (the fruit again reads as gold/wealth), narcissus, and peach or plum blossoms are the traditional purchases — not roses, not generic "spring" arrangements. A florist that stocks the actual plants people are looking for, and says so in plain language, beats one that just puts up red bunting.

**Looking good for the new year.** Getting a haircut before New Year is common — cutting hair during the first several days of the new year is traditionally avoided, so salons see a rush in the week or two before, then a real lull right after. If you run a salon, that's worth scheduling extra chairs for, not extra ad spend during the dead week.

## Doing it right, not just doing it loud

Red and gold decor in your storefront or on your homepage banner is fine as a signal, but it's not marketing on its own — every business on the block will have it. What actually moves people:

- **Google Business Profile posts in the customer's language**, not just English with a red border. A Mandarin or Cantonese post about your reunion-dinner menu, posted well ahead of time, shows up in local search results for people searching in that language.
- **Local Chinese-language press and community calendars.** World Journal and Sing Tao Daily still run real classified and event listings in this area, and community Facebook groups for Monterey Park, Alhambra, and Arcadia get real engagement in the weeks before the holiday. This isn't nostalgia advertising — it's where the audience actually looks.
- **A specific, bookable offer**, not a vague sale. "Reunion dinner menu, book by [date]" beats "Happy Lunar New Year, everyone welcome."

None of this requires a big budget. It requires knowing what the holiday actually is for the people walking into your business, and saying something specific about it instead of hanging a banner and hoping.
