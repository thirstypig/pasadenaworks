> **TL;DR** — The Department of Justice's position is that the ADA's requirements apply to what businesses offer on the web. It also states plainly that it has no regulation setting out detailed technical standards, and that businesses "can currently choose how they will ensure" their online offerings are accessible. Both halves are true at once, which is why nobody can sell you certified compliance, and why doing the obvious work still substantially reduces your exposure.

This subject reaches most business owners as a letter, and the letter is designed to frighten. It is worth separating what is genuinely established from what is being implied by somebody with a commercial interest in your alarm — including, it should be said, some of the people selling remedies.

## What is established

The Department of Justice has been notably direct about the underlying principle, and its guidance states that "the Department has consistently taken the position that the ADA's requirements apply to all the goods, services, privileges, or activities offered by public accommodations, including those offered on the web."

So the threshold question — does this apply to an ordinary business with a website — is answered, and answered against the hopeful reading. If members of the public can buy from you, book with you, or find out what you do, the law's expectations reach that.

California adds its own dimension, which is why the state features so heavily in these conversations: state law provides its own route for accessibility claims alongside the federal one, and that combination is a substantial part of why California sees the volume it does.

## What is not established, and this is the part nobody selling to you will mention

Here the very same guidance is equally direct, and it is quoted considerably less often by the people who cite the first half of it.

"The Department of Justice does not have a regulation setting out detailed standards," it says, and businesses "can currently choose how they will ensure that the programs, services, and goods they provide online are accessible to people with disabilities." On the technical standards everybody cites, it is careful: existing standards such as the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines "provide helpful guidance," which is a materially weaker statement than a requirement.

Read those two sections together and the position is coherent rather than contradictory. The obligation is real and stated in general terms, while the method of meeting it is left to you — which means there is no federal checklist that, once completed, renders a website compliant.

That has an immediate consequence for anything sold to you as certification, because nobody is in a position to certify you against a standard the government has not actually adopted. A vendor may genuinely improve your site, and a badge saying it is compliant is a marketing artifact rather than a legal shield.

## Where overlays fit

The most heavily marketed product in this space is the accessibility overlay — a script added to a site that claims to remediate it automatically.

The claim to examine is not whether overlays do anything, because they do. It is whether adding one resolves the underlying obligation, and the accessibility community has been consistent and vocal that automated remediation does not address a large proportion of the problems that matter, some of which are structural and some of which are about content rather than code.

Treat an overlay the way you would treat any product sold primarily as protection: ask what specifically it fixes, ask what it does not fix, and be suspicious of any answer implying the question is now closed.

## What actually reduces exposure

The unglamorous work is also the effective work, and most of it is neither expensive nor a rebuild.

Images need text alternatives, for the straightforward reason that a screen reader cannot describe a photograph, and color contrast needs to be sufficient — a measurable property, and frequently a matter of adjusting a few values in a theme. Everything usable with a mouse needs to be usable with a keyboard, which is a genuine failure mode on menus and forms. Forms need labels attached to their fields rather than placeholder text standing in for them. Videos need captions.

That list is short, dull, and covers a substantial share of what these complaints actually cite. It is worth doing on its own merits, since the people it helps are also customers, and it happens to be the same list that a serious remediation engagement would begin with.

## The honest summary

You cannot buy certainty here, and anybody offering it is selling something the government has not defined. What you can do is make the obvious fixes, keep a record of the work and when it was done, and avoid the specific trap of believing a purchased widget has closed the matter.

That is a less comfortable position than a certificate on the wall. It is the accurate one, and it happens to be the one the Department of Justice describes.
