> **TL;DR** — There are four common billing models — hourly, fixed-price project, monthly retainer, and a percentage of advertising spend — and each of them creates a specific incentive that works against you in a specific situation. Hourly rewards slowness, fixed price rewards cutting scope, retainers reward doing the minimum that keeps the invoice unquestioned, and percentage-of-spend rewards spending more. None of these is disqualifying; buying one without knowing which conflict it carries is the mistake.

Business owners ask about the rate, which is understandable and slightly beside the point, because the rate tells you almost nothing without the structure it sits inside. A high hourly rate from somebody fast is cheaper than a low one from somebody slow, and both are cheaper than a modest retainer that buys nothing.

## The four models, and what each one quietly encourages

**Hourly** is the most transparent and the most awkward, and you can see exactly what you bought, and the incentive runs the wrong way, since the consultant earns more by working slower and nothing by being efficient. It suits work whose shape is genuinely unknown — an investigation, a mess nobody has diagnosed — and suits it badly once the work becomes predictable.

**Fixed-price project** moves the risk to the consultant, which is generally where it belongs, and creates the opposite pressure: once the price is agreed, everything that can be trimmed is money earned. That is fine when the deliverable is specified precisely and becomes an argument when it is not, and specify the deliverable in writing, in the sort of unglamorous detail that feels excessive at the time.

**Monthly retainer** buys availability and continuity, and it is the model most likely to drift into paying for nothing, and the failure is quiet: work happens for three months, then attention moves elsewhere, and the invoice keeps clearing because nobody wants to have the conversation. A retainer with a named monthly deliverable is a different object from one that buys "ongoing support".

**Percentage of advertising spend** aligns the consultant with your budget growing rather than your results improving, which is a genuine conflict rather than a theoretical one. It is common because it is easy to administer, and if you use it, agree in advance what happens when the correct advice is to spend less.

## What you are actually paying for

Consultants charge for three quite different things, and conflating them is how people end up disappointed.

**Doing the work** — writing the pages, building the campaign, fixing the listings, and this is the most visible and frequently the least valuable, because it is also the most substitutable.

**Knowing what work to do**, which is to say the diagnosis itself, and this is where the money is genuinely earned, and it is the part that looks like a conversation rather than a deliverable, which makes it easy to undervalue. Somebody who tells you your ads are fine and your booking page is the problem has just saved you a year.

**Being accountable** — somebody whose job it is to notice, in October, that the thing you set up in June stopped working. Small businesses buy this without naming it, and then feel they are paying for very little in a quiet month.

Decide which of the three you actually want, and A business that has staff to execute needs the second and third; a business with nobody available needs the first as well, and should expect to pay accordingly.

## The questions that sort a good one from an expensive one

Ask what they would do first and why, before any engagement, and A good answer is specific to your situation and frequently unflattering; a poor one is a list of services.

Ask them what they will not do, since anybody serious has boundaries, and hearing "we do not do social media, and for your business I would not prioritize it anyway" is more reassuring than a full-service list.

Ask how you will know it worked, and listen for whether the measure is a business outcome or a platform metric. Impressions and rankings are inputs; enquiries, bookings and customers are outcomes, and the substitution of one for the other is the most common way a disappointing engagement stays alive.

Ask what happens in the event that it does not work, bearing in mind that the answer is rarely a refund and should not be nothing at all.

## What the work should not include

Some line items are contradicted by the platforms they claim to serve, and those are worth catching before signing.

Google's own documentation states there are "no additional requirements to appear in AI Overviews or AI Mode, nor other special optimizations necessary" and that no new machine-readable files or markup are needed for those features. A proposal charging for AI-specific files as a requirement is charging for something the platform says is unnecessary.

Similarly, local ranking comes down to relevance, distance and prominence by Google's own account, and distance is not something anybody can sell you. A proposal implying otherwise is either uninformed or counting on you being so.

## A reasonable shape for a small business

Most small businesses are best served by a paid diagnosis first — a few hours, fixed price, ending in a written list of what to do in what order — followed by a decision about who executes it.

That structure costs little, produces something you own regardless of what happens next, and tells you a great deal about whether you want to work with this person. A consultant who resists selling a diagnosis, and wants to begin with a twelve-month retainer, has told you which of the four models they are optimizing for.
