> **TL;DR** — WIPO has administered 84,743 domain name disputes since 1999, including 6,282 in 2025 alone, and the formal dispute process exists for the genuinely adversarial cases, which is emphatically not how most businesses actually lose a domain. The ordinary causes are duller and entirely preventable: the renewal notice went to an address nobody monitors, the card on file expired, or the registration was in a supplier's name the whole time and nobody checked until the relationship ended.

The dispute record is worth knowing about because it tells you what the formal remedy looks like, and it is also slightly misleading as a picture of risk, since it only contains the cases somebody escalated.

## What the formal record shows

The World Intellectual Property Organization operates the best-known route for domain disputes under ICANN's Uniform Domain-Name Dispute-Resolution Policy, and its caseload is public.

WIPO has handled **84,743** cases since 1999, with **6,282** filed in 2025, which constitutes a substantial and long-running body of decisions demonstrating two separate things: a formal mechanism genuinely exists, and it is used at a scale that implies the problem is not rare.

What the UDRP addresses is comparatively narrow, however, since it is designed for cases where a domain matching a trademark was registered and used in bad faith — cybersquatting, in the ordinary sense. It constitutes a genuine remedy, it involves considerable cost and evidence, and it takes months rather than days.

## How businesses actually lose domains

Set the disputes aside and the common failures are administrative, which is why they are so avoidable and so frequently missed.

**The renewal notice went nowhere.** The registrar emails the registrant contact, and the registrant contact is a former employee, a closed mailbox, or a web developer who stopped forwarding it, so nobody reads the warning, the domain expires, and the first symptom is that email stops working entirely.

**The card expired.** Auto-renew remains enabled, the card on file does not, and the resulting failure notice arrives at precisely the same unmonitored address described above.

**It was never yours.** The domain was registered by whoever built the site, in their own name, purely as a convenience, and this is not usually malice — it is an afternoon in 2019 when somebody was moving quickly — so it only becomes visible when you want to move, or when they stop answering.

**Somebody registered it after it lapsed.** Once a domain completes its expiry cycle and returns to general availability, anybody may register it, including people who watch expiring names for resale. At that point you are negotiating a purchase, and the UDRP does not help you unless the specific bad-faith conditions apply.

## The check that prevents almost all of this

Look the domain up in a public WHOIS or ICANN lookup and establish three facts, which takes about five minutes.

**Which registrar holds it**, because that is who you actually deal with. **When it expires**, because that converts a vague worry into a dated one — write it in the calendar you actually use, with a reminder a month before. **Whose contact details are on it**, because that is where the warnings go.

Then attempt to log in, because if you cannot log in you do not currently control the domain, whatever the arrangement is generally understood to be, and that is the finding that genuinely matters.

## Fixing what you find

If the registrant turns out to be a supplier, ask them to transfer it; most will do so without difficulty, and the ones who hesitate have told you something genuinely useful about how the relationship will eventually end.

If the contact email is stale, change it to something durable — a shared business address rather than one person's. Enable auto-renew and attach a current card to it, and consider registering for several years simultaneously, which eliminates the annual opportunity for failure.

If it has already lapsed, act inside the earliest recovery window available, because the cost rises sharply at each stage and the last stage is not a stage at all.

## When the formal route is worth it

If somebody else holds a domain matching your trademark and registered it in bad faith, the UDRP exists and its record is public and long.

For most small businesses this is emphatically not the situation, which is instead that a renewal notice was sent to somebody who departed in 2023, and the remedy is consequently a calendar entry rather than a legal proceeding.
