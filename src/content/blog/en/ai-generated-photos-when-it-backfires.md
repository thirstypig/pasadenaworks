> **TL;DR** — Generated images are fine where a stock photograph would have been fine, which means decorative and illustrative use. They backfire wherever the image is doing evidential work: your premises, your team, your finished jobs, your products. A local business is selling proximity and trust, and a picture of a building that does not exist undermines both the moment somebody drives past. Note also that provenance metadata is increasingly attached to generated images, so "nobody can tell" is a weakening assumption.

The temptation is easy enough to understand, given that photography is genuinely expensive, the results from a generator are immediate, and a small business site frequently needs six images it does not have.

The question worth asking is not whether the image looks convincing to a viewer, but rather what that image is being used to claim on your behalf.

## Decorative versus evidential

Sort every image on your site into one of two categories, and the decision becomes straightforward.

**Decorative or illustrative** images carry mood or break up text: an abstract header, a conceptual illustration of a process, a background texture. Nothing about them asserts a fact, and a generated one is no worse than the stock photograph it replaces. Both are entirely generic, which is precisely the point of using either of them in the first place.

**Evidential** images assert something: this is our shop, this is our team, this is a roof we replaced, this is the dish we serve. Here a generated image is not a substitute but a false statement, and the fact that it looks real is precisely what makes it a problem rather than what makes it acceptable.

Almost every regrettable use of generated imagery on a small business site is an evidential image treated as a decorative one.

## Why local businesses lose most from this

A national brand selling a commodity online can decorate a page with anything, because nobody is going to visit its head office.

A local business is in an entirely different trade, because somebody choosing between two dentists in Monrovia is trying to work out what walking through that door will be like, and the photographs are doing most of that work. A generated exterior means the person arriving cannot find your entrance. A generated team means the person who arrives is greeted by strangers. A generated example of your work sets an expectation you will then fail to meet, in front of somebody who came in because of it.

The photograph of your actual building — imperfect, badly lit, with the neighboring shop half in frame — outperforms the generated one, because it does a job the generated one cannot do at any quality level.

## The "nobody can tell" assumption is weakening

Businesses often reason that detection is impossible and therefore the question is moot, and that reasoning is becoming less safe.

Industry provenance standards such as C2PA attach signed metadata describing how an image was produced, and platforms are progressively surfacing that information to viewers. The direction of travel is toward more disclosure rather than less, which means an image published today may be labelled by a platform tomorrow without you doing anything.

Even setting the technology aside, people are becoming rapidly better at spotting it, and the failure is asymmetric. Nobody praises a business for using real photographs; a customer who notices a fake one revises their opinion of everything else on the page.

## Testimonials and faces, specifically

There is one use that moves from unwise to genuinely dishonest, and it deserves naming.

A generated portrait attached to a testimonial fabricates a customer, and a generated face on a "meet the team" page fabricates an employee. Both invent a person to support a claim, both are readily detectable when somebody looks for the person, and both convert an ordinary marketing embellishment into something a reasonable customer would describe as a lie.

If you have testimonials without accompanying photographs, publish them without photographs and let them stand on their content, and if your team consists of three people, show three people.

## Where it is genuinely useful

None of this argues against the tool, and there are real uses in a small business site.

Concept illustrations for a process — how a repair works, what the stages of a project are — are a legitimate and often clearer alternative to a photograph. Background textures and abstract headers are perfectly acceptable, as are mock-ups explicitly labelled as mock-ups, and labelling them costs you nothing beyond a caption.

Whatever the source, the ordinary image work still applies: descriptive alt text, a sensible filename, a compressed file, and a caption where one helps. Google's image guidance is unchanged by how the picture was made, and a generated image with no alt text is inaccessible for exactly the same reason a photograph without it is.

## The rule worth keeping

Use generated images wherever you would previously have used a stock photograph, and use an actual camera everywhere the picture is functioning as evidence of something.

Your telephone is sufficient for the second category, and the modest quality of a real photograph is not the liability people assume it is. It says a person who works here took it, which is the thing the expensive alternative was trying to imitate.
