> **TL;DR** — Say yes, because a prohibition will not be obeyed and will only ensure you stop hearing about it. Then write one page covering three things: what may never go in, what may never come out unchecked, and who approves what before it is published. The business remains responsible for everything published in its name, which is the reason the page exists, and it is a fifteen-minute document rather than a policy project.

An employee who asks this question is doing you a considerable favor, since the alternative version of this conversation is the one in which you find out about it afterwards. Treat the question as a good sign, and answer it with the seriousness it deserves.

## Why a ban is the worst available answer

The instinct to prohibit is entirely understandable, and it fails immediately on contact with reality.

These tools are already on every phone, they cost nothing to use, and the work genuinely does get done faster with them, which together mean that a prohibition mostly produces quiet use rather than no use at all. It also removes your ability to say anything about how they are used, because nobody asks permission for something already forbidden, and you lose the chance to prevent the two or three failures that actually matter.

The businesses that get hurt in this area are not generally the permissive ones at all, but rather the ones where nobody in a position to intervene knew what was happening.

## Rule one: what never goes in

The first rule concerns what an employee may type into one of these tools, and it is the rule that most business owners have never stopped to think about.

Customer information does not go in, and neither do supplier contracts, unpublished prices, employee details, anything covered by a confidentiality agreement, or anything a customer told you in confidence. The reason is straightforward: material entered into a third-party service has left your control, and the fact that it probably will not resurface is not the same as a guarantee, particularly on a free consumer tier.

State this as a short list of concrete examples rather than as a principle, because "use good judgment about confidential information" is not an instruction that anybody can actually follow at three o'clock in the afternoon.

## Rule two: what never comes out unchecked

The second rule concerns what may ultimately be published, and it divides into two parts that are worth separating carefully.

**Facts about the business must come from the business.** Prices, hours, service areas, timescales, credentials, guarantees. An assistant asked for these will produce plausible ones, and plausible is indistinguishable from correct until a customer relies on it. Anything factual is therefore copied from a document that you maintain, rather than generated on demand.

**Claims about outcomes need to be true.** "Most jobs finished same day" is a promise the business will be held to, and it does not become safe because a tool wrote it. Whatever appears in your marketing is a representation by your business under the ordinary advertising rules, and the FTC's guidance in this area does not soften because the sentence was drafted by software.

## Rule three: who approves what

The third rule concerns process rather than content, and it should be kept proportionate to the actual risk rather than allowed to become bureaucratic.

Draw a line between low-stakes material that an employee can publish directly — a social post about opening hours, a reply to a routine enquiry, a reformatted price list — and anything that makes a claim, quotes a price, or represents the business to somebody who has not bought yet. Everything falling into that second category gets read by you before it goes out.

That is a considerably smaller category than it sounds, and keeping it small is precisely what stops the policy from being quietly ignored. A rule that requires approval for everything gets routed around within a fortnight.

## What to say in the actual conversation

Tell them yes, walk them through the three rules above, and ask them to report back afterwards on what the tool actually saved them.

That last part matters considerably more than it initially appears, because an employee who reports "it saved me an hour on the newsletter" is describing a real gain you can build on, whereas one who reports "it wrote our whole services page" is describing something you need to read this afternoon. Asking for that report is the mechanism by which you learn which of the two you are dealing with.

Also worth saying plainly: the writing still has to sound like your business. Google's own guidance on AI content makes the useful distinction here — the objection is not to using the tool but to publishing material with little value for the reader — and generic copy fails that test regardless of who typed it.

## The document itself

The document runs to a single page, with three headings matching the rules above, each carrying three or four concrete examples drawn from your actual business rather than from a template somebody downloaded.

Review the document whenever something material changes, whether that is a new tool, a new service line, or a customer complaint that touched on the subject. This is not a governance program and treating it as one is how it ends up unwritten. It is the fifteen minutes that stops the two failures you would otherwise find out about from a customer.
