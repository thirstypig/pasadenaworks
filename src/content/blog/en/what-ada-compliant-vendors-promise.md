> **TL;DR** — Three claims recur: that a federal standard exists and they can certify you against it, that their product makes you compliant, and that the risk is uniform across the country. The Department of Justice has published its position on the first two and it contradicts them, while the filing counts contradict the third — New York saw 1,021 federal filings in 2025 and California saw four. None of this means the underlying work is optional. It means the pitch and the work are different objects.

A business owner receiving one of these pitches is usually alarmed, occasionally already holding a demand letter, and in no position to evaluate technical claims. The useful defense is not technical knowledge; it is knowing which three claims to check, and where the published answers are.

## Claim one: "we will certify you as compliant"

This is the claim that fails most cleanly of the three, because it depends entirely on a standard that has never been adopted.

The Department of Justice states that it "does not have a regulation setting out detailed standards" for web accessibility, and that businesses "can currently choose how they will ensure that the programs, services, and goods they provide online are accessible to people with disabilities." Of the technical guidelines everybody cites, it says existing standards such as WCAG "provide helpful guidance" — which is a materially weaker statement than a requirement.

Nobody can certify you against a standard the government has declined to set, and A vendor may genuinely improve your site, and the certificate is a document they printed.

## Claim two: "our product makes you compliant"

The second claim fails on the published data rather than on the wording of the law itself.

WebAIM's 2026 survey of one million home pages found six failure types accounting for **96%** of all detected errors: low-contrast text on 83.9% of pages, missing alternative text on 53.1%, missing form labels on 51%, empty links on 46.3%, empty buttons on 30.6% and missing document language on 13.5%.

Read that list and ask what a product installed on top of a site could resolve, and alternative text requires knowing what a picture shows. Form labels require knowing what a field asks for, and an empty button needs a name somebody has to write. These are missing pieces of information, and a layer added over the top does not create information that was never recorded in the first place.

Ask any vendor to say, specifically, which of those six their product resolves and which it does not, and A good one will answer precisely, and the answer will be shorter than the pitch.

## Claim three: "everyone is being sued"

The third claim is the one that actually produces the purchase, and the published filing counts complicate it considerably.

Plaintiffs filed **3,117** website accessibility lawsuits in federal court in 2025, up 27% from 2,452 in 2024, and those were heavily concentrated: New York **1,021**, Florida **961**, Illinois **585**. California federal courts saw **four**, because California appellate courts have held that online-only businesses fall outside the ADA, and the pressure here takes the form of demand letters and state-court claims instead.

So the risk is real and it is not uniform, and a national number quoted at a California business is describing a forum that business is unlikely to end up in. Ask any vendor citing filing counts at you which jurisdiction those particular counts were drawn from.

## What a legitimate engagement looks like

Everything above concerns the pitch, and none of it says the work is unnecessary.

A serious proposal names specific defects on your specific pages, tells you which are quick and which need development, gives you a remediation record that you keep, and does not promise a legal outcome. It is likely to be less exciting and more expensive than a subscription, and it produces something that still exists after you stop paying.

The question that sorts them fastest is the same one that works everywhere else on this site: ask where the requirement is written down. The answer will be a documentation page or it will be a tone, and you will know within a sentence which one you are dealing with.
