> **TL;DR** — Ask every happy customer, right after you've done the work, with a direct link and a short specific text or email. Never offer a discount for a review and never ask only the customers you think will say something nice — Google and Yelp both ban that, and it can get your listing suspended. Yelp goes further and asks businesses not to solicit reviews at all, so treat it differently than Google.

The awkward part isn't asking. It's asking wrong — cornering someone in person, sending five follow-up emails, or bribing them with 10% off. Do it right and most people don't mind at all. You just did work they were happy with. Asking once, cleanly, is the whole job.

## What Google and Yelp actually allow (as of 2026)

Google is fine with you asking for reviews. What it bans is picking who you ask based on how happy they seem, and paying or discounting for one. Google's rules, tightened again this year, spell out specifically:

* No discounts, freebies, or loyalty points for leaving a review — in either direction. That includes offering something to get a bad review changed or taken down.
* No "review gating" — sending a survey first and only routing the happy responses to Google. Every customer you ask gets the same public link.
* No pressuring someone to write a review while they're still standing in front of you, and no telling them what to say or which employee to name.
* No review quotas for staff. "Get five reviews this week" is exactly the kind of internal pressure that produces the fake-sounding reviews Google is trying to catch.

Break these and the penalty isn't a warning email — reviews get stripped and profiles get suspended, increasingly by automated detection rather than a human flagging you.

Yelp is a different animal. Yelp's official position is that businesses shouldn't ask for reviews at all — not "ask carefully," just don't. Yelp's algorithm already downweights or hides reviews it thinks were solicited, and a pattern of asking can get a warning banner slapped on your whole page. If Yelp matters to your business, the move is to make sure you show up well and let reviews come in on their own, not to run an ask campaign there. Save the asking for Google.

## When and how to actually ask

Timing matters more than wording. Ask too early and the customer hasn't formed an opinion yet. Ask too late and the moment's gone.

**The best window is right after the job is done and they've said something positive out loud or in a message.** If a client just texted "this looks great, thank you," that's your opening — not next week.

**Text beats email for most small-business work.** People read texts. Email review requests mostly die in inboxes next to newsletters they never open. If you already text customers about appointments or invoices, send the ask from the same thread.

Here's a script that works because it's short and it doesn't oversell:

> Hey \[name], glad the \[job] worked out. If you have 30 seconds, a Google review really helps other folks in \[city] find us: \[link]. No pressure either way.

For email, the same idea, slightly longer:

> Subject: Quick favor?Hi \[name], thanks again for the chance to work on \[job]. If you were happy with it, a short Google review would mean a lot and helps other \[city] businesses find us. Here's the direct link: \[link]. Takes about a minute. Either way, thanks for the work.

**A QR code works best at the physical point of a good interaction** — a card at checkout, a sticker on a service invoice, a sign near the register. It's low-pressure because nobody's watching them do it, which sidesteps Google's "don't ask on the premises under pressure" line as long as you're not standing there waiting.

One more mechanical note: link directly to your Google Business Profile's review form, not your homepage. Google's "Get more reviews" link in your Business Profile dashboard generates the right one. Anything that makes the customer hunt for where to click loses most of them.

## What to do with a bad review

You will get one eventually, and it's not the disaster it feels like. A page of all five-star reviews with no replies actually reads as less trustworthy than a page with a few honest ones and thoughtful responses.

Reply publicly, briefly, and without arguing. Something like:

> We're sorry this didn't go the way it should have. We'd like to make it right — can you reach out to us directly at \[phone/email] so we can sort this out?

That's it. Don't relitigate the job in the review thread. Don't offer money in the public reply — take that conversation private. If the review violates an actual Google policy (fake, posted by a competitor, contains hate speech, references something that never happened), you can flag it for removal through your Business Profile, but don't count on that working for a review that's just negative and true. You can't incentivize your way out of a bad review, and trying to buy a good one to bury it puts you back in the territory that gets accounts suspended. The honest fix is doing the work well enough that the next twenty reviews outnumber it.
