> **TL;DR** — When a relationship ends badly, both parties usually believe they own the website, and both are partly correct, because a website is actually several assets carrying separate owners. The domain belongs to whoever is registered as the registrant, the hosting belongs to whoever is billed for it, and the design and code belong to whoever the contract identifies — while in the absence of a contract the default position is frequently considerably less favorable to the client than the client assumes. Paying an invoice, by itself, transfers precisely nothing.

The unpleasant realization in these situations is that "we paid for it" constitutes a substantially weaker claim than it feels like, and the useful moment to absorb that is now rather than during the argument itself.

## The four assets, again, because this is where it matters

A website is not a single thing, and a dispute exposes that reality faster than any other circumstance manages to.

**The domain** is settled by the registrant record held at the registrar, which constitutes a fact rather than an opinion and can therefore be looked up. Whoever is listed possesses the practical control, and the formal route for challenging a registration — ICANN's dispute policy, under which WIPO alone has administered 84,743 cases since 1999 — was designed for bad-faith registrations of trademark-matching names rather than for ordinary commercial disagreements.

**The hosting** is settled by whose account and whose card it happens to be, and this one generally resolves quickly, because hosting is inexpensive and replaceable and neither side gains very much by fighting over it.

**The content** — your words, your photographs, your prices — is normally yours in substance, and it is simultaneously sitting on somebody else's server, which constitutes a different problem from ownership.

**The design and code** are the genuinely contested item, and they are governed entirely by the contract.

## What happens without a contract

This is the part that surprises people, and it deserves to be stated plainly rather than reassuringly.

In the absence of a written agreement assigning rights, a client who commissioned the work does not automatically own the underlying design and code outright. What is commonly implied resembles something closer to a license to use it — which is generally sufficient for ordinary purposes and is emphatically not the same as ownership, and the distinction becomes visible precisely when you want to take the work somewhere else.

This is not a settled universal rule and it varies according to jurisdiction and circumstance, which is itself exactly the point: without a contract you are relying on defaults you did not choose and cannot predict. If a genuine dispute is already under way, that is a conversation for somebody qualified rather than for an article.

## What actually decides these

Strip out the feelings and disputes turn on documents, which is why the party holding better records tends to prevail regardless of the underlying merits.

What decides them is the registrant field, the billing records, the contract if one exists, and the written correspondence in which somebody actually agreed to something. Then there is the frequently decisive question of whoever currently holds the login, because possession constitutes a very large fraction of the law's practical effect here, whatever the eventual entitlement turns out to be.

That last consideration explains why the practical advice on this site is always about holding accounts rather than about being right.

## The clause worth having, before you need it

One paragraph in an engagement, agreed while everybody is still cheerful, eliminates almost all of this.

Ask for it in plain terms: on final payment, the client owns the design and content, and the supplier assigns any rights subsisting in the deliverables. Establish that the domain is registered in the client's name, and that the client holds the hosting account with the supplier added as a user. And establish what a handover actually consists of — files, database, and the credentials — together with the requirement that it happens within a stated number of days of a request.

A supplier who agrees to that is describing an entirely normal professional relationship, whereas one who resists is telling you exactly what leverage they intend to retain, and that is worth discovering before you sign rather than afterwards.

## If you are already in one

Do the unexciting things first, and do them in this order.

Establish who the registrant is and whether you can still log in, take complete copies of everything you can currently reach, and gather the invoices and the correspondence into a single place. Then make one plain, dated, unemotional request in writing for whatever you want handed over and by when, and retain a copy of it.

If it involves a genuinely disputed invoice, paying it and then relocating is frequently cheaper than being right, which constitutes unsatisfying advice that nevertheless ends with you holding the asset instead of the argument.
