> **TL;DR** — When a relationship ends badly, both parties usually believe they own the website, and both are partly right, because a website is several assets with separate owners. The domain belongs to whoever is the registrant. The hosting belongs to whoever is billed. The design and code belong to whoever the contract says, and in the absence of a contract the default is frequently less favorable to the client than they assume. Paying an invoice does not by itself transfer anything.

The unpleasant realization in these situations is that "we paid for it" is a weaker claim than it feels, and the useful time to learn that is now rather than during the argument.

## The four assets, again, because this is where it matters

A website is not one thing, and a dispute exposes that faster than anything else.

**The domain** is settled by the registrant record at the registrar, which is a fact rather than an opinion and can be looked up. Whoever is listed has the practical control, and the formal route for challenging a registration — ICANN's dispute policy, under which WIPO alone has administered 84,743 cases since 1999 — is designed for bad-faith registrations of trademark-matching names, not for ordinary commercial disagreements.

**The hosting** is settled by whose account and card it is. This one usually resolves quickly, because hosting is cheap and replaceable and neither side gains much by fighting over it.

**The content** — your words, your photographs, your prices — is normally yours in substance, and it is also sitting on somebody else's server, which is a different problem from ownership.

**The design and code** are the genuinely contested item, and they are governed by the contract.

## What happens without a contract

This is the part that surprises people, and it is worth stating plainly rather than reassuringly.

In the absence of a written agreement assigning rights, a client who commissioned work does not automatically own the underlying design and code outright. What is commonly implied is something closer to a license to use it — which is usually enough for ordinary purposes and is not the same as ownership, and the difference becomes visible precisely when you want to take the work somewhere else.

This is not a settled universal rule and it varies by jurisdiction and circumstance, which is itself the point: without a contract you are relying on defaults you did not choose and cannot predict. If a real dispute is under way, that is a conversation for somebody qualified rather than an article.

## What actually decides these

Strip out the feelings and disputes turn on documents, which is why the party with better records tends to prevail regardless of the merits.

The registrant field. The billing records. The contract, if one exists. The written correspondence in which somebody agreed to something. And, frequently decisive, whoever currently holds the login — because possession here is a very large fraction of the law's practical effect, whatever the eventual entitlement.

That last one explains why the practical advice on this site is always about holding accounts rather than about being right.

## The clause worth having, before you need it

One paragraph in an engagement, agreed while everybody is cheerful, removes almost all of this.

Ask for it in plain terms: on final payment, the client owns the design and content, and the supplier assigns any rights in the deliverables. State that the domain is registered in the client's name. State that the client holds the hosting account and the supplier is added as a user. And state what a handover consists of — files, database, and the credentials — and that it happens within a stated number of days of a request.

A supplier who agrees to that is describing a normal professional relationship. One who resists is telling you what leverage they intend to keep, and that is worth knowing before you sign rather than afterwards.

## If you are already in one

Do the unexciting things first, in this order.

Establish the registrant and whether you can log in. Take complete copies of everything you can currently reach. Gather the invoices and the correspondence into one place. Then make one plain, dated, unemotional request in writing for what you want handed over and by when, and keep it.

If it involves a genuinely disputed invoice, paying it and then moving is frequently cheaper than being right, which is unsatisfying advice that ends with you holding the asset instead of the argument.
