> **TL;DR** — Business listings are partly crowd-maintained, which means somebody who is not you can suggest that your hours changed, that you permanently closed, or that your category is different, and some of those suggestions go live before you are told, while reporting a listing is similarly open to anybody at all. The realistic defense is not prevention but speed: turn on notifications, look at your own listing weekly from a signed-out phone, and keep the evidence that would support a reinstatement before you need it.

Owners discover this in one of two ways: either enquiries stop for a week and somebody eventually searches the business and finds it marked permanently closed, or a customer mentions that the hours online were wrong. Both discoveries arrive too late.

## Why the listing is editable by strangers

This surprises a great many people, and it constitutes an entirely deliberate design choice rather than an oversight.

Map data improves when the public can correct it, because businesses move, close and change hours far faster than any company could survey. The cost of that openness is that the mechanism does not know the difference between a helpful passer-by and an interested party.

The practical consequence is that your listing is not a document you own outright but a record to which you are the primary though certainly not the only contributor, and treating it as set-and-forget is precisely the mistake.

## The three things that actually happen

**Suggested edits.** These cover hours, address, category, website, or the business name itself, and some changes require review while others appear almost immediately, and the ones that hurt most are the quiet ones — a phone number that is one digit different, hours that are subtly wrong on Saturdays.

**Marked permanently closed.** This is the genuinely severe one, because the listing does not disappear so much as actively instruct people not to come, and a business can lose a fortnight of trade to it before anybody notices.

**Reported for a guideline violation.** Anybody can report a listing, and if the report has any surface plausibility — a name containing keywords, a service-area business with an address shown, a home-based business — it may land you in exactly the review process discussed in the suspension article, which is slow and evidence-driven.

## The defense, which is mostly noticing

You cannot stop strangers from suggesting edits, so the entire game is the gap between a change happening and you knowing.

Turn on notifications for the profile and ensure they arrive at an address somebody actually reads, and add a second manager so the business does not depend entirely on one person's inbox. Then look at the listing yourself once a week, from a phone, signed out, because that is the version customers see and it occasionally differs from the dashboard.

Put a recurring reminder in whatever calendar you genuinely use, since weekly is entirely sufficient and the inspection itself takes approximately thirty seconds.

## What to do when something has changed

Correct it immediately and then document it, in precisely that order rather than the reverse.

Restore the correct information through the profile, though take a screenshot of the incorrect state first if you still can, because it becomes genuinely useful if a pattern subsequently develops. If the listing was marked closed, correct the status and expect it to take a little time to propagate.

If the profile has been suspended rather than edited, Google documents the reinstatement route, and it is an evidence submission rather than an argument. Complete it once, attach photographs of your signage and premises, a utility bill or lease in the business name at that address, and your registration. Do not submit repeatedly and do not create a second listing while you wait, since that turns one problem into two.

## If it keeps happening

A single incorrect edit is ordinary background noise, whereas a pattern is an entirely different matter, and the appropriate response is documentary rather than confrontational.

Keep a dated log containing screenshots of each individual incident, and do not accuse a competitor publicly, and do not respond in kind, because the second is prohibited conduct in its own right and the first is how a business ends up explaining itself to people who were not paying attention.

The other honest defense is to render yourself considerably less plausible as a target: a business name that matches your signage and registration, a category that genuinely matches what you do, an address that is accurate about whether customers can visit. These are the things that make a malicious report fall apart at review, and every one of them is under your control.
