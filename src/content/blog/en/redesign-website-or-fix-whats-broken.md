> **TL;DR** — Most sites do not require a redesign at all. They require two or three specific things repaired: a slow page, a confusing menu, a contact form failing silently. A complete redesign is worth undertaking only where the site is genuinely broken on mobile, built on something nobody can maintain any longer, or where the brand itself has actually changed. Get this wrong and a careless redesign can destroy the rankings your current site has already earned.

If you are asking whether you need an entirely new website, the honest first question is what is actually wrong with the one you currently have. Nine times out of ten, when a business owner says they need a redesign, what they mean is that the site embarrasses them or that it is not producing leads, and neither of those is automatically a redesign problem. Sometimes it genuinely is a redesign problem, although far more usually it amounts to two or three entirely fixable things wearing a redesign costume.

## When a full redesign is actually the right call

There are genuine reasons to rebuild the whole thing, and they are reasonably specific:

**The site is genuinely broken on mobile, rather than merely unattractive.** Mobile devices have accounted for the majority of global web traffic since 2016, and [in North America they run around 57%](https://kinsta.com/mobile-vs-desktop-market-share/), so more than half your visitors are probably holding a phone. If text overflows the screen, buttons are too small to tap accurately, or the menu simply does not function on mobile, that is not a fixable widget but the entire layout failing at the job it exists to perform, and patch jobs on a non-responsive site rarely hold up for long.

**It is built upon something nobody can realistically maintain.** If your site runs on a discontinued platform, a theme receiving no further updates, or hand-coded HTML from years ago that exactly one person ever understood — and that person has since departed — then you are not maintaining a website so much as conducting archaeology every time anybody touches it. At some point rebuilding on something current costs less than the accumulated hours spent working around what you already have.

**The brand has genuinely changed.** A new name, a new logo, new services, or a pivot toward an entirely different customer are all reasons to start again, and if the site still describes a business you are no longer operating, that is not something a tweak resolves.

**Conversion is performing poorly everywhere, rather than on one particular page.** If visitors land on the site and depart from every page rather than one confusing one, and you have already eliminated traffic quality and pricing as explanations, then the entire experience may genuinely be the problem.

If none of those four conditions genuinely holds in your case, continue reading before signing any redesign contract whatsoever.

## When it's actually just 2-3 things

Here is the pattern we encounter constantly: a business requests an entirely new website, and when we actually examine what exists, the real list turns out to be short.

* **The site is slow.** The usual causes are large unoptimized images, a bloated theme, or an excess of plugins, and this is frequently a few hours of work rather than a rebuild, because compressing images and trimming whatever loads unnecessarily can drop load time from eight seconds to two.
* **One page is confusing.** Ordinarily this is the homepage or the services page, where visitors arrive, cannot determine what you do or how to purchase it, and consequently leave; repairing a single page's layout and copy is a day of work rather than a redesign.
* **The photographs are outdated.** A stock photograph from years ago, or a team photograph missing three people who have since left. Replacing the photographs is an afternoon of work rather than a redesign of anything.
* **The contact form is broken.** It is either not submitting at all, or it is delivering to an inbox nobody monitors. This is the single most expensive bug on a small business site, because every lead arriving through it simply vanishes, and it ordinarily takes under an hour to repair once somebody actually locates it.

Redesigns run anywhere from a few thousand dollars for a small brochure site to well into five figures for anything more custom, whereas targeted repairs addressing the list above ordinarily run from a few hundred dollars to low four figures in total. If your actual problem appears on that list, you are paying redesign money in order to repair a form.

We would considerably rather tell you that you do not need us for six weeks but do need us for two days than sell you the larger job.

## The real risk: a careless redesign can wreck your SEO

If you genuinely do need the rebuild, one particular mistake costs businesses real money afterward: altering URLs without redirecting the old ones.

Every page on your current site that ranks in Google, even modestly, has search engines and other websites pointing at that exact URL. A new site carrying a new URL structure and no 301 redirects from the old addresses discards all of that accumulated standing, because Google must then discover the new pages from scratch and re-earn the trust the old ones established across months or years. Meanwhile anybody searching the phrase that formerly brought them to you, or clicking an old link from another site, arrives at a dead page.

This is entirely avoidable and it is not exotic. Before a redesign goes live, list every URL the current site holds, map each one to wherever its content now lives, and establish a 301 redirect for every single one, including pages you are deleting outright, which should point at the closest relevant replacement. [Google's own site-move documentation](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes) is specific about two points that get missed: do not redirect many old URLs to one irrelevant destination such as the homepage, because that confuses users and may be treated as a soft 404, and keep the redirects in place for at least a year rather than removing them once the new site feels settled. Any developer performing a redesign should treat this as non-negotiable rather than as an afterthought, and if whoever is building your new site has not raised it, ask them about it before signing anything off.

A redesign done properly does not cost you your rankings, whereas a redesign done carelessly can comfortably cost you six months of them.
