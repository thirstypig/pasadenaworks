> **TL;DR** — Most sites don't need a redesign. They need two or three specific things fixed: a slow page, a confusing menu, a contact form that silently fails. A full redesign is worth it only when the site is broken on mobile, built on something nobody can touch anymore, or the brand has actually changed. Get this wrong and a careless redesign can tank the rankings your current site already earned.

If you're asking "do I need a whole new website," the honest first question is: what's actually wrong with the one you have? Nine times out of ten, when a business owner says "we need a redesign," what they mean is "our site embarrasses me" or "it's not bringing in leads." Neither of those is automatically a redesign problem. Sometimes it is. Usually it's two or three fixable things wearing a redesign costume.

## When a full redesign is actually the right call

There are real reasons to rebuild, and they're specific:

**The site is broken on mobile, not just ugly.** More than half of your traffic is probably on a phone. If text overflows the screen, buttons are too small to tap, or the menu doesn't work at all on mobile, that's not a fixable widget — that's the whole layout failing at the job it has to do. Patch jobs on a non-responsive site rarely hold up.

**It's built on something nobody can maintain.** If your site runs on a platform that's been discontinued, a theme with no more updates, or hand-coded HTML from years ago that only one person ever understood — and that person is gone — you're not fixing a website, you're doing archaeology every time you touch it. At some point rebuilding on something current costs less than the hours spent working around what you have.

**The brand has actually changed.** New name, new logo, new services, a pivot to a different customer. If the site still describes a business you're not running anymore, that's not a tweak.

**Conversion is bad everywhere, not on one page.** If people land on the site and leave from every page — not just one confusing one — and you've already ruled out traffic quality and pricing, the whole experience might genuinely be the problem.

If none of those four are true, keep reading before you sign a redesign contract.

## When it's actually just 2-3 things

Here's the pattern we see constantly: a business asks for "a whole new website," and when we actually look, the real list is short.

- **The site is slow.** Big unoptimized images, a bloated theme, too many plugins. This is often a few hours of work, not a rebuild — compress images, trim what's loading that doesn't need to, and load time drops from eight seconds to two.
- **One page is confusing.** Usually the homepage or the services page — visitors land, can't tell what you do or how to buy it, and leave. Fixing one page's layout and copy is a day of work, not a redesign.
- **The photos are outdated.** A stock photo from years ago, a team photo missing three people who left. Swap the photos. This is not a redesign, it's an afternoon.
- **The contact form is broken.** It's not submitting, or it's going to an inbox nobody checks. This is the single highest-cost bug on a small business site because every lead through it just disappears, and it usually takes under an hour to fix once someone finds it.

Redesigns run anywhere from a few thousand dollars for a small brochure site to well into five figures for something more custom. Targeted fixes for the list above usually run a few hundred to low four figures total. If your actual problem is on that list, you're paying redesign money to fix a form.

We'd rather tell you "you don't need us for six weeks, you need us for two days" than sell you the bigger job.

## The real risk: a careless redesign can wreck your SEO

If you do need the rebuild, there's one mistake that costs businesses real money afterward: changing URLs without redirecting the old ones.

Every page on your current site that ranks in Google — even modestly — has search engines and other sites pointing to that exact URL. A new site with a new URL structure and no 301 redirects from old URLs to new ones throws all of that away. Google has to discover the new pages from scratch and re-earn the trust the old ones built up over months or years. In the meantime, anyone who searches the phrase that used to bring them to you, or clicks an old link from another site, hits a dead page.

This is avoidable and it's not exotic. Before a redesign goes live: list every URL the current site has, map each one to where its content lives on the new site, and set up a 301 redirect for every single one — including pages you're deleting outright, redirected to the closest relevant replacement. Any web developer doing a redesign should treat this as a non-negotiable step, not an afterthought. If whoever's building your new site hasn't brought this up, ask them about it before you sign off.

A redesign done right doesn't cost you your rankings. A redesign done carelessly can cost you six months of them.
