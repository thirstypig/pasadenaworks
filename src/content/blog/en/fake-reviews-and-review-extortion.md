> **TL;DR** — The FTC's 2024 final rule prohibits six things, and the two that matter most to an honest small business are these: you may not condition compensation or incentives on a review "expressing a particular sentiment, either positive or negative", and you may not use threats, intimidation or false accusations "to prevent or remove a negative consumer review". Asking every customer for an honest review remains entirely permissible, whereas offering a discount for a five-star one does not, and neither does telling a reviewer you will sue unless they remove it.

Most coverage of this rule is aimed at businesses buying reviews in bulk, which is not the audience here. The parts worth knowing are the ones that catch a business acting in good faith and reaching for the obvious response.

## What the rule covers

The Federal Trade Commission announced a final rule in August 2024 banning six categories of conduct, and it carries civil penalty authority, which is the part that changed the stakes.

The six are: **fake reviews and testimonials**, including ones that misrepresent who wrote them or falsely claim the writer used the product, and explicitly including AI-generated fake reviews; **incentivized reviews** conditioned on a particular sentiment; **undisclosed insider reviews** by officers, managers, employees or agents who do not disclose the connection; **fake review websites** a company controls while presenting them as independent; **review suppression** through threats, intimidation or false accusations; and **fake social media metrics**, meaning bought followers or views from bots or hijacked accounts.

Read that list and notice how much of it is not about buying reviews at all.

## The trap for an honest business: incentives

The rule prohibits "providing compensation or other incentives conditioned on the writing of consumer reviews expressing a particular sentiment, either positive or negative."

The load-bearing words are *conditioned on* and *particular sentiment*. A raffle entry for anybody who leaves a review, positive or negative, is a fundamentally different object from a discount for a five-star review, because the first asks for participation while the second purchases an opinion.

That distinction also settles a question small businesses ask constantly, because asking every customer for a review, in the same words, at the same point in the visit, is entirely acceptable and always has been. Asking only the customers who seemed happy constitutes review gating, which platforms already prohibit, and paying for the happy ones is now a matter for the FTC.

## The trap that catches people at their angriest: suppression

The second provision is the one that surprises owners, because it prohibits the instinctive response to an unfair review.

You may not use threats, intimidation or false accusations to prevent or remove a negative consumer review. That covers telling a reviewer you will sue them, implying you will report them for something, or claiming falsely that they were never a customer in order to get the review removed.

This is worth internalizing before it is relevant, because the moment it becomes relevant is the moment judgment is worst. A review that is genuinely defamatory, or that violates a platform's own content policy, has a proper route: report it to the platform under the policy it breaches. Google publishes what it prohibits, and a review containing personal attacks, off-topic content or conflicts of interest can be flagged on those grounds.

What you may not do under any circumstances is contact the reviewer directly and apply pressure.

## What is left, and it is most of what works

Strip out the prohibited approaches and the effective ones remain untouched, which is the reassuring part of this.

Ask everybody, consistently, using identical wording, at the same moment, and make it straightforward with a short link. Respond to negative reviews briefly and without hostility, since the response is read by everybody who arrives later and is genuinely more valuable than the removal you wanted. Fix whatever the review is actually about, and say so in the response when you have.

None of that is genuinely slower than the shortcuts; it is simply less satisfying during the week the bad review appears.

## If somebody is extorting you

Businesses do receive messages demanding payment to remove or avoid a negative review, and the correct response is documentary.

Do not pay, do not argue publicly, and preserve the message. Report it to the platform, which treats extortion as a policy violation rather than a review dispute, and report it to the FTC, since a scheme of this description is exactly what the rule now reaches.

That is a slower answer than paying, and paying identifies you as somebody who pays.
