> **TL;DR** — Searches that end without a click are not an invention of AI, and for a local business they were never the valuable ones. What deserves attention is whether the questions that used to bring somebody to your site — the comparing, the deciding, the checking whether you seem trustworthy — still do. Google's generative AI report counts impressions and not clicks, so measuring this precisely is not currently possible, which makes the outcomes you can measure more important rather than less.

The phrase doing the rounds is "zero-click," and the fear behind it is straightforward: if a search engine answers the question outright, nobody visits your website, and a website that nobody visits cannot do anything for you.

The fear is a reasonable one, and it is also considerably older than the technology now being blamed for it, which is why thinking clearly about precisely which questions you have lost is worth a great deal more than a general anxiety covering all of them at once.

## This began long before AI answers

Google has been answering questions on the results page for well over a decade, and it did so without much commentary at the time.

Opening hours, telephone numbers, addresses, the weather, the conversion between one currency and another, the year a film was released — all of these have been resolved directly on the results page for years, often inside a panel assembled from a business's own listing. If your hours appear in somebody's search results and they never visit your website, that transaction was completed in 2016 as readily as it is completed now.

What that history establishes is useful, because those searches did not damage local businesses when they stopped producing visits. The person who wanted your closing time got your closing time, and if they liked what they saw they came to the shop. A visit to your website was never the point; it was a step on the way to something else, and removing a step is not the same as removing the destination.

## Which questions were genuinely worth a visit

The distinction that matters is between questions with a single factual answer and questions that end in a choice.

Nobody comparison-shops an opening hour. Somebody deciding which of four dentists to register with, however, or which shop to trust with a repair costing several hundred dollars, is engaged in something that an assembled answer does not finish on their behalf — they want to see the place, which in practice means photographs resembling the actual premises rather than stock images, reviews reading as though real people wrote them, prices where you are willing to publish them, and some evidence that you will treat them decently.

Those are the visits that were ever worth having, and they are conspicuously not the ones a summary resolves: a summary can establish that four dentists exist within a reasonable distance, but deciding between them is precisely the work it hands back to the person asking.

So the sharper version of the worry is not whether people are clicking less. It is whether you are among the businesses being named at the point where the choosing starts, and whether what they find when they do look holds up.

## The measurement problem, stated honestly

There is a genuine limitation here, and it is better to know about it than to be sold around it.

Google's Search Console does report on generative AI features, but that report counts impressions — the number of times links to your site were shown inside such a feature — and it does not report clicks or average position. It also combines AI Overviews and AI Mode into a single figure rather than separating them.

The practical effect is that you can see Google was willing to show your page inside an AI answer, and you cannot see from that report whether it produced a visit. Anybody offering to prove that their work increased your clicks from AI features is offering something the available reporting does not currently support.

## What to watch instead

Since the channel itself cannot be isolated cleanly, watch the things that were always the real measures, and watch them over quarters rather than weeks.

Telephone calls and direction requests recorded on your Google Business Profile are the most useful signals available to most local businesses, precisely because they capture somebody acting on a decision without ever touching your website. If those hold steady while raw sessions fall, you have lost visits that were not converting anyway, which is a different and much less alarming problem than losing customers.

Beyond that, ask arriving customers how they found you, and keep the answers somewhere. It is unfashionably manual, and for a business with a few dozen customers a month it is more reliable than any dashboard, because it captures the whole path rather than the last step of it.

## What actually follows from this

Very little of what you should do changes, which is usually the case when a genuine shift gets described in catastrophic terms.

Keep the factual questions answered accurately wherever they get answered — your listing, your site, the directories that still carry you — because being the source of a correct answer is how you get named at all. Put the effort into the pages that serve a decision rather than a fact, since those are the visits that were ever worth having. And stop treating raw session count as the health of the business, because it has been a decreasingly good proxy for a decade and this is merely the most recent reason.

The businesses hurt by this shift are largely the ones whose traffic was always informational and never commercial. If somebody's living depended on being the page that told strangers what time it gets dark, that is a real loss. For a business that people choose and then visit, the decision was never made on your website; it was only informed there, and it still is.
