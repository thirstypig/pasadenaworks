> **TL;DR** — The Pew Research Center tracked the actual browsing of 900 US adults through 68,879 Google searches in March 2025, of which 12,593 produced an AI summary. Where a summary appeared, users clicked a traditional result on 8% of visits, against 15% where none appeared. Clicks on a link inside the summary happened on 1% of visits. And sessions ended more often on pages with a summary: 26% against 16%. Those four numbers are the most honest picture available of what AI search does to a small business's traffic.

Almost everything written about AI search is either a vendor's projection or a publisher's complaint. This is neither: it is observed behavior from people who agreed to have their browsing recorded, which makes it worth more than the rest of the genre combined.

## What the study measured

The Pew Research Center analyzed data from **900 US adults** who consented to sharing their browsing activity, across the period **1–31 March 2025**.

Within that window the dataset contained **68,879 unique Google searches**, of which **12,593** produced an AI summary — roughly one search in five, or 18% overall. Around six in ten respondents, **58%**, encountered at least one.

That is a real methodology with a stated sample, a stated period and a stated volume, which is precisely what the vendor material in this area lacks.

## The four findings that matter to you

**Clicks on results roughly halve.** Users who encountered an AI summary clicked a traditional search result on **8%** of visits. Users who did not encounter one clicked on **15%** — nearly twice as often.

**Almost nobody clicks inside the summary.** A click on a source link within the AI summary occurred on just **1%** of visits to pages carrying one. This is the finding that should reset expectations, because a great deal of advice currently sold is aimed at being cited inside those summaries.

**Sessions end more often.** Browsing ended on **26%** of pages with an AI summary, against **16%** of pages showing only traditional results. The answer satisfied the person and they stopped.

**It is not yet most searches.** At 18%, AI summaries appeared on fewer than one search in five during the study period. That is substantial and it is not the whole of search, and a proposal treating it as the whole of search is overstating the case.

## What this means for a local business

The first temptation is to read this as catastrophe, and the second is to read it as irrelevant, and both are wrong in the same way — they ignore what kind of query is involved.

A summary is most likely to satisfy somebody asking a general informational question, which is exactly the kind of article traffic that was never going to convert well anyway. A person searching for a plumber who can come today, a restaurant open now, or a price for a specific service still needs to reach somebody, and a summary does not complete that transaction.

So the reasonable response is not to stop writing but to change what you write. Content whose whole value was answering a general question is worth less than it was. Pages that answer a question only you can answer — your prices, your availability, your specific process, the thing about your neighborhood — are worth what they always were, because a summary cannot substitute for them.

## How to read your own numbers against this

The pattern in your Search Console will be the one described here, and knowing that in advance stops it being misread.

Stable impressions with declining clicks is the expected signature, not evidence that something broke. Split it by query if you can: informational terms losing clicks while commercial ones hold is the study's finding reproduced in your own account, and it tells you where to put effort rather than telling you to panic.

And treat the 1% figure as the correction it is. Being cited inside a summary is worth having and it is not worth restructuring your business around, because on the published evidence it produces a visit one time in a hundred.
