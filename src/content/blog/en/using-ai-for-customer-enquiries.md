> **TL;DR** — Sort your enquiries into three groups before automating anything: the ones with a single correct answer, the ones needing judgement, and the ones where being wrong is expensive. Only the first group is safe to automate, and it is usually a larger share of the total than owners expect. Whatever an assistant says on your behalf is a statement by your business, so it should be built from your own written answers rather than from whatever the model believes about your industry.

The complaint about automated replies is almost always described as a problem of tone, and tone is genuinely the least consequential part of it. A reply that sounds slightly stiff but nonetheless answers the question is perfectly acceptable, whereas a reply that sounds warm and tells somebody the wrong opening hours has cost you a customer and quite possibly a review as well.

The useful work therefore happens before any drafting begins, in deciding what a machine is permitted to say on your behalf at all.

## Sort the enquiries first

Spend an hour reading through your last hundred enquiries and place each one into one of three groups, because that initial sorting determines everything that follows from it.

**Group one: a single correct answer exists.** Are you open on Sunday, where do I park, do you take that insurance, how long does the repair usually take, do you deliver to Arcadia. These are questions where an answer is either right or wrong and you already know which, and they are frequently sixty per cent or more of the total.

**Group two: judgement is required before answering.** Can you fit me in this week, is this particular item covered under what we already agreed, would you recommend the cheaper option given my situation. These require somebody who knows the current state of the business, and an assistant attempting to answer them will produce something that is plausible and owned by nobody.

**Group three: expensive to get wrong.** This covers anything involving a complaint, a refund, a legal or medical question, or a person who is already unhappy with you. Automating these constitutes a false economy, since the cost of a bad answer is not a single enquiry but the entire relationship, and quite possibly a public review as well.

Automate the first group, route the second to whoever can exercise the judgement it requires, and put the third in front of a person immediately.

## Build the answers from your own words

The single most important implementation decision concerns where the answers themselves come from, and the common mistake consists of letting them come from the model's general knowledge of the world.

Write your genuine answers to the group-one questions into a single document, covering your actual hours, your actual service area, your actual turnaround, and the three things you do not do at all. Then have the assistant answer *from that document* rather than from what it knows about businesses like yours. This is the difference between a system that says what you actually say and a system that says something merely reasonable about your industry, and only the first of those is safe to put in front of customers.

Keep that document as the single place where those facts live, and update it whenever the underlying facts change. It doubles as the source for your website's own copy, which is a useful side effect rather than an accident.

## Tone, briefly

Once the content itself is correct, the tone problem becomes a comparatively small one, and it is mostly solved by three straightforward instructions.

Give it two or three real examples of how you actually write, since imitation from concrete samples works considerably better than an abstract adjective such as "friendly". Ban the vocabulary you would never use yourself, which for this site means "solutions", "leverage" and "empower", and for yours will mean whatever particular words make you wince. And keep replies short, because length is what makes automated writing recognizable more than any individual word does.

## Say that it is automated

An automated first reply should say so plainly, in a single clause, together with an indication of when a person will follow up on it.

The argument for this is not merely an ethical one, since a customer who knows they are reading an automated acknowledgement reads it charitably and waits; a customer who believes a person wrote it, and then notices the seams, concludes that your business is careless or evasive. The disclosure costs you nothing whatsoever, and it buys the reply a degree of patience that it would not otherwise have received.

Bear in mind too that anything your automated system asserts is a representation by your business, held to the same standard as anything on your website or in an advertisement. If it promises a timescale, that is a promise; if it states a price, that is a price. The FTC's advertising and marketing guidance is the ordinary frame here, and it does not soften because a machine produced the sentence.

## Where the honest limits are

Do not automate anything whose output you will not subsequently be able to examine, because if a system replies to customers and you never read the transcripts, you have effectively outsourced your reputation to something you are not supervising, and the first sign of trouble will be a review.

Read a sample of them every week for the first month, and thereafter whenever your business changes anything a customer might ask about, whether that is prices, hours, services or staff. The characteristic failure mode of these systems is not that they sound robotic, but that they confidently repeat something which stopped being true back in March.
