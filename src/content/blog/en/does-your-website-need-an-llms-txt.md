> **TL;DR** — No, not for Google, and Google says so in its own documentation rather than leaving it to be inferred: you do not need to create AI text files, machine-readable files, or special markup to appear in its AI features. The file is a proposed convention that no major search or assistant provider currently requires. If somebody has quoted you a monthly fee to add one, the file is not the problem — the pricing of a text file as an ongoing service is.

Every few years a new file appears that a certain kind of consultant will offer to sell you, and the current one is called `llms.txt` — proposed to sit at the root of your domain much as `robots.txt` already does, on the argument that it tells AI systems how to understand your site.

The question worth answering is narrower than the debate around it: does a small business need one? For the largest system that matters to most local businesses, the answer is documented, and it is no.

## What Google actually says

Google's documentation on AI features is unusually blunt on this point, and it is worth quoting rather than summarizing, because the summary is what gets contested.

There are, Google writes, "no additional requirements to appear in AI Overviews or AI Mode, nor other special optimizations necessary." It then names the category directly: "you don't need to create new machine readable files, AI text files, or markup to appear in these features. There's also no special schema.org structured data that you need to add."

An AI text file at the root of your domain is the thing that sentence describes. The requirement Google does state is the ordinary one — a page "must be indexed and eligible to be shown in Google Search with a snippet" — which is the same bar it has always been.

That does not settle every question about the file's future, and it does settle the one you are being sold on.

## What the file is supposed to do

The underlying idea is not absurd, which is a large part of why it has spread as quickly as it has.

A website is built for a person with a browser, and much of it is navigation, styling, and repeated furniture that carries no meaning. The proposal is that a site could publish a plain-text summary of itself — what it is, which pages matter, where the substance lives — so that a system reading it does not have to infer all of that from markup.

As an idea about machine-readable structure that has a long and entirely reasonable history behind it, the proposal is defensible; what it conspicuously lacks at present is adoption by the systems that would have to honour it. A convention accomplishes something only once the reader has agreed to read it, which means a file that no major provider has committed to using is, for the moment, a file that simply sits there.

## Where the money is

The gap between "harmless proposal" and "recurring invoice" is where this stops being a technical question.

An `llms.txt` file is, in the end, a text file: writing a sensible one for a small business site is perhaps twenty minutes of work performed once, and it carries no ongoing maintenance beyond an edit on the rare occasions when your site changes substantially.

A proposal to add one is therefore not objectionable in itself. A proposal to charge you monthly for having added one deserves the question you would ask about any recurring charge, which is what specifically happens next month that justifies it. If the honest answer is that the file continues to exist, you are paying rent on twenty minutes of typing.

It is also worth watching for the bundling, which is where most of the money in this actually sits. It is common for a genuinely useful engagement — fixing a site that is slow, hard to crawl, or full of contradictory business details — to be presented under an AI-optimization heading, because that sells more readily in the current climate than the same work described accurately. The work underneath may well be worth paying for, but it is old work addressing old problems, and it ought to be priced and explained as such rather than reframed to match whatever is currently generating anxiety.

## The honest case for adding one anyway

There is a genuinely defensible argument for adding the file, and it is worth stating precisely because it bears no resemblance to the one appearing in the sales material.

The cost is close to nothing, which is the beginning and very nearly the end of the argument. If the convention is eventually adopted by systems that matter, a site that already has a sensible file is marginally ahead, and if it is never adopted, you have spent twenty minutes and a few hundred bytes. Writing one also forces a genuinely useful exercise, which is stating plainly what your site is and which pages actually matter — a question a surprising number of businesses find difficult, and the difficulty is usually diagnostic of something worth fixing.

So: add one if you like, or have whoever maintains your site add one while they are doing something else. Do not buy it as a product, do not accept it as evidence that a retainer is working, and do not let it displace the work that is documented to matter.

## What to do instead, in the same twenty minutes

If you have a short window and would like it spent well, begin by reading your own `robots.txt` and confirming that nothing in it blocks the crawlers determining whether you can be quoted at all. Then search your business name to establish whether your site is indexed in the first place, since nothing downstream matters if it is not. Finally, open your Google Business Profile alongside your contact page and reconcile whichever details disagree between them.

Each of those three checks addresses something documented to affect whether you appear at all, whereas the file remains speculative — and while it is perfectly reasonable to do the speculative thing cheaply, it is not reasonable to be charged for it as though it were the documented one.
