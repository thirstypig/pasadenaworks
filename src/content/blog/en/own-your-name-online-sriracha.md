What the company does hold is the green cap and the rooster. The lesson for a small business is not about hot sauce: the descriptive name you picked because it explains what you do is the hardest thing to own, and the assets you can actually hold are the domain, the profile and the handle. Register those this week.

Most advice about protecting a business name arrives from somebody selling a trademark filing, which is not wrong and is usually not the first thing. The more useful place to start is a well-documented case where a company built something enormous and did not own its name, because it makes the distinction between the two kinds of asset unusually clear.

## What actually happened

David Tran's company — based in Irwindale, a few miles east of Pasadena — built a hot sauce that became a category, and it did so without ever registering the word on the label.

Tran was dissuaded from securing a trademark on the word *sriracha* on the basis that it is difficult to obtain one named after a real-life location — the sauce takes its name from Si Racha, a coastal city in Thailand. The US Patent and Trademark Office treats "sriracha" as a generic term, and the consequence is exactly what you would expect: other manufacturers make and sell products called sriracha, and Huy Fong cannot stop them.

What the company does hold, and holds securely, is the distinctive part of the packaging, and the green cap and the rooster logo are trademarked. Those are the elements a competitor cannot copy.

## The distinction worth carrying

The useful split is between a name that describes what you do and a name that identifies who you are, because the law treats them very differently and so does the internet.

A descriptive or geographic name is the one small businesses instinctively choose, because it explains the business immediately: Pasadena Plumbing, Valley Dental, Foothill Auto. That instinct is commercially reasonable and it produces the hardest kind of name to protect, since you are asking to own words that everybody in your trade has a legitimate reason to use.

A distinctive name — invented, arbitrary, or a mark rather than a word — is harder to explain and far easier to own. Most small businesses will not switch, and should not on this basis alone, and what they should do is understand which kind they have, because it determines what protecting the name even means.

## What you can actually own, this week

Whatever your name's legal position, there is a short list of things that are yours if you register them and somebody else's if you do not, and the entire list is available at low cost.

**The domain**, registered in the business's name, on a card the business controls, and consider the obvious variants — the hyphenated one, the `.net`, the common misspelling — not because they are valuable but because they are cheap and awkward in somebody else's hands.

**The Google Business Profile**, claimed and verified under your own control rather than a supplier's, and this is the one most often left unclaimed, and it is frequently worth more than the website.

**The social handles**, even on platforms you do not intend to use, and an account you never post to still stops somebody else from being you.

**The obvious search result.** If a competitor advertises on your business name, you cannot stop that, and it is worth knowing rather than discovering.

That list is an afternoon and a modest annual cost, and it is the practical meaning of "owning your name" for almost every business reading this.

## When a trademark is genuinely worth it

None of this argues against registration, and there are situations where it plainly earns its cost.

If your name is distinctive rather than descriptive, if you are expanding beyond one location, if you sell a product rather than local services, or if somebody has already started trading under something close to your name, a filing is a serious conversation to have with somebody qualified. This article is emphatically not that conversation, and it is not a substitute for one.

What is worth knowing before it is that a registration protects a name; it does not retrieve a domain you never registered, and it does not claim a Business Profile you left unverified.

## The part Huy Fong got right

The story is usually told as a cautionary tale, and it is only half of one, because the company remained enormously successful without owning the word.

Tran has described the imitators as free advertising, which is a defensible position when your bottle is recognizable across a room. That recognizability is the asset, and it was built deliberately: a specific cap, a specific logo, a specific bottle. Those are ownable, and they are what the company registered.

The small-business version of that is unglamorous but exact, and you may not be able to own "Pasadena Plumbing". You can own the domain, the profile, the handles and the reputation attached to them, and those are the things that a competitor with a similar name cannot take.
