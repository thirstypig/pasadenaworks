> **TL;DR** — A patient arriving at a practice website is trying to answer four questions: are you taking my insurance, are you taking new patients, where exactly do I park, and how do I reach a human. A site that answers those four plainly outperforms a far handsomer one that buries them. Separately, each physician in a practice may hold their own Google listing alongside the practice's — Google's guidelines say so explicitly — and that is frequently worth more than anything on the website itself.

The websites of small medical practices tend to fail in a peculiar way. They are rarely ugly, because a template took care of that, and they are rarely broken. They simply do not answer the questions that brought somebody to them, which is a harder problem to notice, since nothing on the page looks wrong.

The useful exercise, consequently, is to stop evaluating the site as a piece of design and begin evaluating it as a set of answers to questions somebody arrived already holding.

## The four questions

Somebody looking for a doctor is running a short checklist, mostly in a hurry, and frequently on a telephone in a car park.

**Do you take my insurance?** This is first for a large share of patients and it decides whether the rest of the visit happens at all. A page listing the plans accepted, dated, with a plain sentence saying to ring and confirm because networks change, answers it honestly. A site that says "we accept most major insurance plans" has answered nothing, and it has additionally taught the reader that this practice is vague about money.

**Are you taking new patients?** This wants a yes or a no, stated on the page and dated, which practices consistently resist on the grounds that the answer changes from month to month. The alternative, however, is that everybody telephones to ask, and the accumulated cost of those calls to your front desk is considerably greater than the cost of amending a single line of text whenever the position shifts.

**Where do I actually park?** This sounds trivial until you consider how many practices sit in medical office buildings with a structure round the back, validation at a desk, and an entrance that is not the one the map pin suggests. Two sentences of accurate description at this point will prevent a genuine number of late arrivals, and consequently a genuine number of appointments that overrun into somebody else's.

**How do I reach a human?** What is wanted here is a telephone number rendered as text rather than as an image, so that a mobile browser will dial it when somebody taps it, and a contact form standing alone does not substitute for one. A patient holding an urgent question is not going to complete a form and then wait, which means that a site offering only a form has quietly filtered out precisely the enquiries that were most likely to become appointments.

## The part that outranks the website

For a practice with a physical location, the Google Business Profile frequently drives more first appointments than the site does, and it has a feature that many practices never use.

Google's guidelines for representing a business treat an individual practitioner as its own entity: "An individual practitioner is a public-facing professional, typically with their own customer base," and doctors are named explicitly among them. Practitioners "may have separate pages" alongside the organisation's, and a profile may include a title or degree.

The practical consequence is that a three-physician practice may reasonably hold four listings — the practice and each doctor — rather than one. Patients frequently search a physician by name, particularly after a referral, and the name search is the one where an absent listing costs the most.

This is not a manipulation of the system, and the guidelines impose real constraints that are worth reading carefully before creating anything at all. The point is simply that a legitimate mechanism exists for making individual physicians findable under their own names, and that most small practices leave it entirely unused.

## What matters less than people expect

A redesign is usually not the answer, because the questions above are content problems rather than design problems, and a new template will reproduce all of them in a nicer typeface.

Stock photography of models in white coats is worse than a plain page, since patients recognise it instantly and it makes a real practice look like a directory listing. A photograph of the actual building — the door they will walk through, the sign they are looking for — is worth more than any of it, and it doubles as directions.

Long biography pages describing a philosophy of care are read by very few people, whereas the same page listing where somebody trained, what they treat, and which languages they speak is read closely. Language is worth calling out specifically in the San Gabriel Valley, where it is frequently the deciding factor and is frequently missing from the page.

## The unglamorous technical half

Two failures interrupt contact between a practice and its patients more often than anything else does, and both share the awkward property of remaining completely invisible until somebody eventually complains about them.

The first is a contact form that silently fails. Practice sites are often several years old, built by somebody no longer involved, and a form that stopped delivering will keep displaying a cheerful confirmation to every patient who uses it. Sending yourself a test message once a quarter takes approximately a minute, and it is probably the single highest-return check available to a practice that depends on enquiries arriving reliably.

The second is speed on a telephone connection. Practice sites accumulate — an appointment widget, a chat box, a review carousel, a tracking script somebody added — and each addition costs load time on the device most patients actually use.

## Where to start on Monday

Open your own site on your telephone while standing outside your own building, which is roughly the circumstance most patients are in, and attempt to answer the four questions at the top of this article while timing yourself against each one.

Whatever took longest is the thing to address first, and it will almost certainly turn out to be insurance or new-patient status rather than anything a designer would have flagged during a review. Having established that, claim the practitioner listings for each physician, and then send yourself a test enquiry through your own contact form to confirm that it still arrives somewhere a person reads.

None of that constitutes a project in the sense that would require a quotation or a schedule. It is an afternoon of unremarkable work, and it will almost certainly return more than a rebuild costing several thousand dollars would.
