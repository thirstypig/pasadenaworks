> **TL;DR** — A high price is not evidence of anything on its own, and a low one is not either. The reliable tests are structural: can you say what last month's money bought, does the work described match what the platform's own documentation says is necessary, and do you hold the accounts you are paying to have maintained. A supplier failing all three of those tests is expensive at very nearly any price.

Business owners tend to ask this question quietly, usually because they already suspect the answer and would prefer not to have offended somebody who has been perfectly pleasant throughout two years of working together.

It is an entirely fair question, and it deserves a better answer than a figure, because the figure is genuinely the least informative component available: two businesses paying identical monthly amounts may be receiving excellent value and absolutely nothing respectively.

## Price is a weak signal

The instinct is to compare what you pay against what somebody else pays, and it fails immediately, because websites are not a commodity and monthly retainers cover wildly different things.

A retainer might cover hosting, security updates, content changes, monitoring, and somebody who answers the telephone when a form stops working. It might also cover a hosting account that costs a few dollars and nothing else whatsoever. Both are described identically on an invoice, and the difference between them is not visible in the number.

The useful questions, therefore, are not about the amount at all, but about what that amount is actually attached to.

## Test one: can you say what last month bought?

Ask your supplier what they actually did during the previous month, and pay attention to whether the answer they give is specific or merely categorical.

A good answer names specific things that happened: a plugin updated, a page changed, a form repaired, a backup verified, a report actually read by somebody. It may well be short, and a short honest answer is fine — some months genuinely are quiet, and a supplier who says "nothing needed doing, here is the uptime record" is being straight with you.

The answer to be alert to is the one that describes a category rather than an action. "Ongoing optimization," "continued maintenance," and "monitoring your rankings" are not statements about last month; they are statements about the shape of the arrangement. If you cannot get past that after asking twice, you are not being told, and there is usually a reason.

## Test two: does the claim survive the documentation?

This is the sharpest test available to you, principally because it does not depend on your own judgement about value at all. It depends on whether the thing being sold is described as necessary by the people who run the platform.

Google's documentation on its AI features is a useful example, since it is where a great deal of current selling happens. It states that there are "no additional requirements to appear in AI Overviews or AI Mode, nor other special optimizations necessary," and specifically that "you don't need to create new machine readable files, AI text files, or markup to appear in these features." An invoice line for AI-specific files or markup, sold as necessary to appear in those features, is contradicted by the vendor's own published position.

That does not automatically make the supplier dishonest, and it very often means somebody sold *them* a methodology which they are now passing on in complete sincerity — but sincere and correct are different things, and what you are paying for is correct.

Apply that same test everywhere else it fits: when something is described to you as required, ask where it is written down as required, and see whether the answer is a documentation page or a confident tone.

## Test three: do you hold the accounts?

This third test is not concerned with price at all, and it matters considerably more than either of the two preceding it.

If you pay monthly for a site whose domain is registered to your supplier, on hosting billed to their card, with a Google Business Profile they own, then you are not a client so much as a tenant. The price could be entirely reasonable and the position would still be poor, because the arrangement is difficult to leave and difficult-to-leave arrangements do not tend to stay reasonable.

Ask which accounts stand in the business's name, and note that a supplier who moves them across without fuss has told you a great deal about their own confidence in the value of what they provide.

## What a fair arrangement looks like

There is no universally correct number here, although there are recognizable structural shapes worth identifying.

You should be able to say, in one sentence and without looking anything up, what you are paying for; you should hold the accounts yourself; and the work described should be work the platform's own documentation actually supports. And leaving should be possible — irritating, perhaps, but possible — without anything being held.

If all four are true, the price is very likely fair even if it is higher than somebody else's. If none of them are, the price is too high even if it is low, because you are buying an arrangement rather than a service, and the arrangement is the expensive part.
