> **TL;DR** — If you sell a handful of products or services and most orders are custom, local pickup, or repeat customers, you don't need an online store. A simple order form or a payment link costs almost nothing to run and takes a day to set up. Build a real store only if you're shipping many different products to strangers who need to browse and compare before they buy.

Somebody told you that you need "an online store," and you believed them, because that's what everyone says a business needs now. But "online store" means something specific: inventory tracking, a shopping cart, shipping calculations, and a catalog you keep updating. A lot of small businesses don't have any of that going on. They have five services and a phone that rings. For that business, a real e-commerce platform is expensive overkill — a form that collects the order and a link that takes the payment does the same job for a fraction of the cost and none of the maintenance.

## What a real online store actually costs you

People price out Shopify's $39/month sticker and think that's the number. It isn't close.

Shopify's Basic plan runs about $39/month billed monthly ($29/month if you commit to a year), and that's before payment processing, apps for anything beyond the basics, and a paid theme if the free ones don't fit your brand. WooCommerce looks free because the plugin is, but you're paying for WordPress hosting ($15–100+/month), a theme, and usually a maintenance service once you don't want to be the one patching plugins — realistic total cost for a real WooCommerce store runs $1,800 to $15,000+ a year depending on how much you need it to do. Square's tiers are cleaner but the same shape: free to start, $49/month for Plus, $149/month for Premium, plus card processing on every sale either way (2.9% + $0.30 online is standard across all of these platforms).

None of that is a rip-off. It's the right price for what it buys: real-time inventory across sale channels, cart recovery for abandoned checkouts, tax calculation by jurisdiction, shipping rate lookups, and a catalog system built to hold hundreds of SKUs. If you're actually selling forty products to people who've never heard of you and need to compare options before buying, that machinery earns its keep.

If you sell six things, or every order is a little different, or you already know most of your customers by name — you're paying full freight for a warehouse when you need a shelf.

## The framework: four questions

Answer these honestly before you pick a platform.

**How many distinct products or services do you actually sell?** Not variations — distinct things. A bakery with three cake sizes and two flavors is still basically one product with options. A boutique with 200 SKUs across ten categories is a catalog. Under about a dozen genuinely different offerings, a form beats a store almost every time.

**Do you ship to strangers, or is it pickup and local delivery?** Shipping is where e-commerce platforms justify their cost — they calculate rates, print labels, and track packages automatically. If everything's picked up at your shop or dropped off within a few miles, you don't need any of that. A pickup time and an address field on a form does it.

**Do you need real-time inventory?** If you're selling a physical product where two people could plausibly try to buy the last one at the same moment, you need real inventory tracking or you'll oversell it. If you're selling a service, a made-to-order item, or something with no hard unit count, "sold out" isn't a problem you have.

**Is most of your business repeat or custom orders, or is it browsing strangers?** A store is built for someone who's never met you to land on a page, compare options, and self-serve a purchase. If most of your revenue is a regular calling to reorder the same thing, or a custom quote you price by hand anyway, the browsing-and-comparing part of a store is wasted on you — you already have the relationship.

If you answered "few," "pickup," "no," and "repeat or custom" to those four, stop pricing out Shopify.

## What the simpler version actually looks like

An order form is a page with fields: what they want, when they want it, how they're paying or picking up, contact info. It submits to your email or a spreadsheet. Pair it with a payment link — Square, Stripe, PayPal, or Venmo for Business all generate one for free — and you can invoice or charge on the spot without building checkout logic yourself. Total setup cost: a few hours of a developer's time, or an afternoon if you're comfortable with a form builder yourself. Ongoing cost: nothing beyond whatever percentage the payment processor takes on each transaction, which you'd pay under Shopify or Square too.

A request-a-quote form works the same way for anything priced by the job — landscaping, catering, custom furniture, cleaning contracts. You're not pretending to have fixed prices you don't have, and you're not building a cart for products that don't have a cart-shaped relationship to begin with.

The honest failure mode we see is businesses that build the full store because it feels more legitimate, then let the catalog go stale because updating twelve products across a real e-commerce admin is more upkeep than they budgeted for. A form doesn't go stale. It just asks the question again every time.
