> **TL;DR** — If you sell a handful of products or services, and most orders are custom, local pickup, or repeat customers, then you do not need an online store. A simple order form or a payment link costs virtually nothing to operate and requires approximately a single day to establish. Build a genuine store only if you are shipping many different products to strangers who need to browse and compare before purchasing.

Somebody told you that you need an online store, and you believed them, because that is what everybody says a business requires now. But "online store" means something reasonably specific: inventory tracking, a shopping cart, shipping calculations, and a catalog you continuously maintain. A considerable proportion of small businesses have absolutely none of that machinery operating. What they typically have instead is five services and a telephone that rings. For that business, a genuine e-commerce platform constitutes expensive overkill, because a form collecting the order and a link taking the payment performs the identical function at a fraction of the cost and with none of the maintenance.

## What a real online store actually costs you

People price out Shopify's advertised monthly sticker and assume that constitutes the number. The realistic operating figure is not remotely comparable to it.

Shopify's Basic plan runs somewhere in the region of $30 to $40 monthly depending on whether you commit annually, and their published pricing has moved more than once — check [Shopify's own pricing page](https://www.shopify.com/pricing) rather than trusting any figure quoted in an article, this one included. Whatever the sticker says, it precedes payment processing, apps for anything beyond the basics, and a paid theme if the free ones do not suit your brand.

WooCommerce appears free because the plugin genuinely is, but you are paying for WordPress hosting, running roughly $15 to $100 or more monthly, plus a theme and usually a maintenance service once you decide you do not want to be the person patching plugins. A realistic total for a genuine WooCommerce store runs from about $1,800 to $15,000 or more annually, depending on how much you require it to do.

[Square's tiers](https://squareup.com/us/en/pricing) are cleaner but the same shape: free to begin, $49 monthly for Plus, and $149 monthly for Premium, plus card processing on every sale regardless of tier. Online card processing sits at 2.9% plus 30 cents, which is standard across essentially all of these platforms.

None of that constitutes a rip-off, because it is the correct price for what it purchases: real-time inventory across sales channels, cart recovery for abandoned checkouts, tax calculation by jurisdiction, shipping rate lookups, and a catalog system constructed to hold hundreds of SKUs. If you are genuinely selling forty products to people who have never heard of you and who need to compare options before buying, that machinery comfortably earns its keep.

If you sell six things, or every order differs slightly, or you already know most of your customers by name, then you are paying full freight for a warehouse when what you actually need is a shelf.

## The framework: four questions

Answer each of these questions honestly before committing to any particular platform whatsoever.

**Approximately how many genuinely distinct products or services do you actually sell?** The question concerns genuinely distinct offerings rather than superficial variations upon them. A bakery offering three cake sizes and two flavors still amounts to essentially one product with options, whereas a boutique carrying 200 SKUs across ten categories constitutes a catalog. Below roughly a dozen genuinely different offerings, a form beats a store almost every time.

**Do you ship to strangers, or is your business pickup and local delivery?** Shipping is precisely where e-commerce platforms justify their expense, because they calculate rates, print labels, and track packages automatically. If everything is collected at your shop or delivered within a few miles, you require none of that, and a pickup time alongside an address field on a form accomplishes it.

**Do you genuinely require continuously updated real-time inventory tracking?** If you are selling a physical product where two people could plausibly attempt to buy the last one simultaneously, you need genuine inventory tracking or you will oversell it. If you are selling a service, a made-to-order item, or anything without a hard unit count, then "sold out" is not a problem you actually have.

**Is most of your business repeat or custom orders, or is it browsing strangers?** A store is constructed for somebody who has never met you to arrive on a page, compare options, and self-serve a purchase. If most of your revenue comes from a regular telephoning to reorder the same thing, or from a custom quote you price by hand regardless, then the browsing-and-comparing apparatus of a store is wasted on you, because you already possess the relationship.

If you answered "few," "pickup," "no," and "repeat or custom" to those four, then you can safely stop pricing out Shopify altogether.

## What the simpler version actually looks like

An order form is a page containing fields: what they want, when they want it, how they are paying or collecting, and contact information. Submissions arrive directly in your email inbox or accumulate in a spreadsheet. Pair it with a payment link — Square, Stripe, PayPal, and Venmo for Business all generate one without charge — and you can invoice or charge immediately without constructing checkout logic yourself. Total installation expenditure amounts to a few hours of a developer's time, or an afternoon if you are comfortable with a form builder. Ongoing expenditure amounts to nothing beyond whatever percentage the payment processor takes per transaction, which you would pay under Shopify or Square equally.

A request-a-quote form operates identically for anything priced by the job — landscaping, catering, custom furniture, cleaning contracts. You are not pretending to hold fixed prices you do not have, and you are not constructing a cart for products that never had a cart-shaped relationship to begin with.

The honest failure mode we encounter is businesses that build the complete store because it feels more legitimate, and then permit the catalog to go stale, because updating twelve products through a genuine e-commerce admin turns out to be more upkeep than they budgeted for. A form, by contrast, does not go stale at all; it simply asks the same question again every single time.
