> **TL;DR** — Work outward from what you control rather than inward from what you have lost. Establish which registrar holds the domain and whether you can log in, since that is the only genuinely irreplaceable item. Request ownership of your Google Business Profile, where the current owner has three days to respond before you may be able to claim it. Assume nothing is malicious until it plainly is, because most of these situations are somebody overwhelmed rather than somebody hostile.

It usually begins on a considerably smaller scale than a crisis: an email goes unanswered for a week and then a fortnight, a number rings out, and the site is meanwhile still up and still working, so there is no emergency exactly and the whole thing is easy to leave for another month.

Leave it long enough, however, and it becomes an emergency on somebody else's schedule — a renewal date, an expired card, a hosting invoice nobody is watching — which is why the useful moment to act is precisely while nothing appears to be obviously wrong.

## First, sort the situations that look alike

Three quite different things produce the same silence, and they need different responses.

Somebody may simply be **overwhelmed**, which is far and away the most common explanation, because small suppliers get ill, take on too much, or quietly wind down without telling anybody, and the work simply stops. Somebody may have **left the business** — a one-person operation that closed, an agency that was acquired, an employee who moved on and whose accounts nobody inherited. Or somebody may be **holding your assets deliberately**, usually over a genuine or imagined unpaid invoice.

The third possibility is genuinely rare and it is nonetheless the one almost everybody assumes first, which matters because assuming it early tends to produce an aggressive opening message, which is precisely what turns a recoverable situation into a dispute. Send a plain, dated, unemotional email stating what you need and by when, and keep a copy. If this ever becomes formal, that message is the beginning of your record.

## Establish the domain position

The domain is the only item on the list that cannot simply be rebuilt, so it goes first regardless of how the rest looks.

A public WHOIS or ICANN lookup will tell you which registrar it lives at and when it expires. That expiry date is the thing to write down, because it converts a vague worry into a deadline. If you can log in to an account at that registrar, you are in a considerably stronger position than you feared, and the sensible next step is to make sure the billing card on file is yours and current.

If you cannot log in, the registrar itself is the next call rather than your developer. Registrars have processes for exactly this, and inter-registrar transfers are governed by ICANN's published transfer policy, which also provides a complaint route when a transfer is refused improperly. Expect this to be slow and documentary rather than fast, which is another argument for starting before the renewal date rather than after it.

## Recover the Google listing, which is quicker than people expect

For a business with a physical location, the Google Business Profile is frequently more commercially valuable than the website, and it has the most tractable recovery process of anything on this list.

Google documents a route for requesting ownership from whoever currently holds a profile. The current owner is notified by email and has three days to respond, and if they do not respond, you may have the option to claim the profile, although Google notes the option is not always available. Three days is a genuinely short window compared with everything else here, and it costs nothing to start.

Do this early rather than leaving it until last, because a profile still pointing at the correct address and telephone number keeps the business running while the slower items are being resolved.

## Preserve what exists before anything changes

While the site remains available, take comprehensive copies of everything on it: save the pages as they render, download the photographs, copy the text into a document, and export anything the site collects — enquiries, subscribers, bookings.

This feels excessive right up until the hosting lapses without notice, at which point a site can simply stop existing. Recreating a page from material you deliberately saved is a comparatively small job, whereas recreating it from memory, or from whatever a search engine happens to have cached, is a considerably larger undertaking.

## Then decide whether you are rebuilding or recovering

Once the domain is secured and the listing is yours, the pressure drops enough to make an actual decision, and it is worth making deliberately.

Recovering the existing site makes sense when it works and the only real problem was the person maintaining it. Rebuilding makes sense more often than people expect, particularly when the site is several years old, when nobody can edit it without the missing person, or when you were unhappy with it anyway and inertia was the only reason it survived. A site you cannot change is a liability regardless of how it looks, and the version of this situation people regret is the one where they spent months recovering something they did not want.

## The thing that prevents the next one

Once this is settled, put the accounts into the business's own name: the domain registered to you, the hosting billed to your card, the Google profile owned by you, and whoever performs the work added as a user or manager on each of them.

That arrangement will not prevent a supplier from disappearing, because no arrangement available to you can accomplish that. It reduces their disappearance from a crisis to an inconvenience, which is the most any arrangement can offer, and it is available for the cost of an afternoon while everybody is still on good terms.
