> **TL;DR** — This was planned as a summary of published agency rate surveys. The search for them is the finding: nearly every "industry rate survey" in marketing is published by an agency, a directory that sells agency listings, or a tool vendor, and almost none states a sample size, field dates or selection method. Compare that with a real survey — the NFIB's technology survey names 521 respondents, dates of 6–31 March 2025, and how they were selected — and the difference is immediately visible. Rather than repeat unsourced numbers, here is how to get a real answer for your own situation.

The honest version of this article is shorter than the planned one and considerably more useful, because a number you cannot check is worse than no number: it anchors your expectations without informing them.

## What went wrong with the plan

The intention was to read the published rate surveys and report what they say, on the principle that an argued figure beats an anecdote.

What surfaced instead was a genre. The "2026 agency pricing report" is typically produced by a company that sells to agencies or takes commission for matching businesses to them, and the incentives run in an obvious direction. More importantly, the documents rarely state how many agencies responded, over what period, or how they were chosen — which means the figures cannot be evaluated at all, favorably or otherwise.

That is not an accusation of dishonesty. It is an observation that this material is marketing with a chart in it, and it should be read as marketing.

## What a real survey looks like, for contrast

Set one of these against an actual piece of research and the difference stops being abstract.

The NFIB Research Center's small business technology survey states that it was fielded by email to a random sample of 20,000 owners from its membership database, ran from 6 to 31 March 2025, and produced 521 completed responses across a named industry mix. It even permits a caveat to be stated — membership samples skew toward established employer firms — which is only possible because the method was published.

Every one of those elements is missing from the typical rate survey. When you next see a pricing statistic in this industry, look for those four things. Their absence is the answer.

## Why nobody publishes a good one

It is worth understanding why this gap exists, because it explains why waiting for better data is not a strategy.

Agency pricing is genuinely not comparable across engagements. A "monthly retainer" can mean six hours of senior time or thirty hours of junior time; a "website" can mean a five-page template or a bespoke build with integrations. Any honest survey would have to normalize scope before it could compare price, and scope is the part nobody standardizes.

The result is that a median figure, even one honestly collected, would describe an average of incommensurable things. That is a real methodological problem rather than a conspiracy, and it means the answer has to be produced locally rather than looked up.

## How to get a real number for your own case

Three quotes, one scope, written down. That is the entire method and it outperforms any survey.

Write a one-page description of what you want — the pages, the functions, who supplies the content, what happens after launch — and send the identical page to three suppliers. The variance you get back is your actual market rate, and it will be wide, which is itself informative.

Then ask each of them the three questions from the pricing article on this site: what does this cost over three years including everything, what do we still have if we stop, and what are you not going to do. A quote that answers all three is comparable with another quote that answers all three, which is the comparison the surveys were failing to give you.

## The general lesson

When an article promises to tell you what something costs, check whether it says who was asked and when.

That check takes ten seconds and it disqualifies most of what is published about pricing in this industry. It is also the same check that makes the good sources — the government data, the membership surveys, the academic work — visibly better, which is a useful thing to be able to see.
