> **TL;DR** — This was planned as a summary of the published agency rate surveys, and the search for them turned out to be the finding: nearly every "industry rate survey" in marketing is published by an agency, by a directory that sells agency listings, or by a tool vendor, and almost none of them states a sample size, field dates or a selection method. Set that genre against an actual piece of research — the NFIB's technology survey names 521 respondents, field dates of 6–31 March 2025, and the procedure by which those respondents were selected — and the difference becomes immediately visible. Rather than repeat figures that nobody can verify, what follows is the method for generating a defensible answer for your own situation.

The honest version of this article is considerably shorter than the planned one and correspondingly more useful, because a number you cannot verify is actually worse than no number at all: it anchors your expectations without informing them.

## What went wrong with the plan

The intention was to read the published rate surveys and report whatever they say, on the entirely reasonable principle that an argued figure outperforms an anecdote.

What surfaced instead was a genre. The "2026 agency pricing report" is characteristically produced by a company that either sells to agencies or collects a commission for matching businesses to them, and the incentives consequently run in an obvious direction. More importantly, these documents rarely disclose how many agencies responded, over what period, or by what method they were selected — which means the resulting figures cannot be evaluated at all, favorably or otherwise.

That is not an accusation of dishonesty, but an observation that the material is advertising with a chart inside it, and it deserves to be read as advertising.

## What a real survey looks like, for contrast

Setting one of these documents against an actual piece of research is what stops the difference from remaining abstract.

The NFIB Research Center's small business technology survey states that it was administered by email to a random sample of 20,000 owners drawn from its membership database, that it ran from 6 to 31 March 2025, and that it generated 521 completed responses distributed across an identified industry mix. It even permits a caveat to be registered against it — membership samples skew toward established employer firms — which is only possible because the methodology was published in the first place.

Every one of those elements is absent from the characteristic rate survey. The next time you encounter a pricing statistic in this industry, look for those four things, because their absence is itself the answer.

## Why nobody publishes a good one

Understanding why this gap exists is worth the effort, because it explains why waiting for better data does not constitute a strategy.

Agency pricing is genuinely incomparable across engagements. A "monthly retainer" can signify six hours of senior attention or thirty hours of junior attention, and a "website" can signify a five-page template or a bespoke build carrying half a dozen integrations. Any honest survey would therefore have to normalize scope before it could compare price, and scope is precisely the variable that nobody standardizes.

The consequence is that a median figure, even one collected with complete honesty, would describe an average of incommensurable things. That is a genuine methodological problem rather than a conspiracy, and it establishes that the answer has to be generated locally rather than looked up.

## How to get a real number for your own case

Three quotes, one scope, written down: that is the entire method, and it outperforms any survey currently in circulation.

Write a one-page description of what you actually want — the pages, the functions, who supplies the content, what happens after launch — and then send the identical page to three separate suppliers. The variance you get back constitutes your genuine market rate, and it will be wide, which is itself informative.

Then ask each of them the three questions from the pricing article on this site: what does this cost over three years including everything, what do we still possess if we stop, and what are you not going to do. A quote that answers all three is comparable with another quote that answers all three, and that comparison is exactly what the surveys were failing to deliver.

## The general lesson

Whenever an article promises to tell you what something costs, check whether it establishes who was asked and when.

That verification takes ten seconds and it disqualifies the majority of what gets published about pricing in this industry. It is also the same verification that makes the good sources — the government data, the membership surveys, the academic literature — visibly better, which is a genuinely useful thing to be able to perceive.
