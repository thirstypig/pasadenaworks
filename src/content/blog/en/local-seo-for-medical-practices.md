> **TL;DR** — Google states that local results are ordered by relevance, distance and prominence. Distance is fixed by your lease and relevance is largely a matter of filling in your profile completely, which leaves prominence as the only factor genuinely worth working on over time. For a medical practice that means claiming a listing for each physician as well as the practice, keeping the same name and address everywhere it appears, and treating reviews as an ongoing operational habit rather than a campaign.

Search marketing sold to medical practices tends to arrive wrapped in vocabulary designed to make the mechanism sound proprietary. The mechanism is published, it is short, and reading it changes what you would sensibly spend money on.

## The three factors, and which one you control

Google's own documentation on improving local ranking names relevance, distance and prominence as the basis for local results, and it is worth taking each one literally.

**Relevance** is how well your profile matches what somebody searched for. This is mostly an exercise in completeness: the correct primary category, the secondary categories that genuinely apply, the services listed, the hours accurate including holidays. It is unglamorous and it is largely finishable — there is an end state, and once you have reached it there is little more to extract.

**Distance** is how far you are from the searcher or from the area they named. You cannot change this without moving, and any vendor implying otherwise is selling something that does not exist. It is worth understanding because it explains a result that otherwise looks like failure: a practice can be genuinely well optimized and still not appear for somebody searching from six miles away, because a nearer practice satisfied the query adequately.

**Prominence** is how well known the practice is, drawn from information Google gathers across the web, and it includes review count and score. This is the only one of the three that responds to sustained effort, which is why it deserves most of the attention.

## Claim a listing for each physician

The single most commonly missed opportunity for a multi-physician practice is that Google's guidelines treat an individual practitioner as an entity in their own right, entitled to a profile alongside the organization's.

An individual practitioner, the guidelines say, is "a public-facing professional, typically with their own customer base," and doctors are named among the examples. That matters because referral traffic behaves differently from symptom traffic: a patient handed a physician's name searches that name, and if nothing authoritative comes back, the practice has lost a patient who had already decided to come.

Read the limits before creating anything, since the guidelines are specific about who qualifies and about not creating listings for people who have left. A listing for a physician who departed two years ago is worse than no listing, because it sends patients toward somebody who cannot see them.

## Consistency is dull and it matters

The practice's name, address and telephone number should appear identically everywhere they appear — your own site, your Google profile, the insurance directories, the hospital affiliation pages, the specialty directories nobody remembers signing up for.

Medical practices are unusually prone to drift here, because listings accumulate through insurers and hospital systems rather than through anybody's decision. Suite numbers appear and disappear, a legal entity name competes with the name on the door, and an old answering service number survives in a directory that still ranks.

The work is tedious and finite. Write down the canonical version of the three fields, then correct what you find, starting with the directories that actually appear when you search your own practice name.

## Reviews, without the parts that get practices in trouble

Review count and score feed prominence directly, and healthcare is also the category where the temptation to cut corners is highest and the consequences worst.

Ask every patient, at the same point in the visit, using the same words, and make it easy by handing over a short link. What you must not do is select which patients get asked based on how the visit went, offer anything of value in exchange, or write reviews yourself — the first is review gating, the others are straightforwardly prohibited, and platforms are considerably better at detecting patterns than practices expect.

Respond to negative reviews briefly, without confirming that the person was ever a patient. This is the point where a practice most often creates a privacy problem while attempting to defend its reputation, and a short, neutral reply inviting the person to call the office is both the safest response and the one that reads best to everybody else.

## What to expect, honestly

None of this produces a result in a fortnight, and prominence in particular accrues over quarters rather than weeks.

The realistic outcome for a small practice that does the above is appearing reliably for searches within its own immediate area, and appearing for its physicians by name. What it will not do is place a single-location practice above a hospital system for a broad specialty term across a whole county, and any proposal implying otherwise should be read carefully for what it actually commits to.
