> **TL;DR** — In several San Gabriel Valley cities, around two in five residents speak Chinese at home, which makes this the ordinary case rather than a niche one. The part most practices get wrong is the order: a translated page helps somebody decide to call you, but what determines whether they become a patient is who answers that call, in which language, during which hours — and whether the paperwork waiting for them exists in a language they read.

## The number to use, and the one to avoid

Most articles on this subject reach for the Asian share of a city's population, and that figure is the wrong variable twice over, because it describes race rather than language while simultaneously grouping together households that run in Mandarin, Cantonese, Korean, Vietnamese and English alike.

What you need is how many residents **speak Chinese at home**, which the Census reports separately in table C16001:

| City | Speak Chinese at home |
|---|---|
| [San Marino](https://data.census.gov/table/ACSDT5Y2024.C16001?g=160XX00US0668224) | 43.8% |
| [Monterey Park](https://data.census.gov/table/ACSDT5Y2024.C16001?g=160XX00US0648914) | 42.1% |
| San Gabriel | 40.1% |
| [Arcadia](https://data.census.gov/table/ACSDT5Y2024.C16001?g=160XX00US0602462) | 37.6% |
| Alhambra | 33.0% |

*U.S. Census Bureau, American Community Survey 2024 five-year estimates, table C16001, residents aged five and over.*

Roughly two residents in five, across five separate cities — which means a practice operating in any of them while working only in English addresses a minority of its immediate neighbors in the language they actually use at home. That is an unusual position for an enterprise whose entire product is being understood correctly the first time.

## The distinction the table hides, and the telephone exposes

Here is the detail that determines everything downstream, and it is a limitation of the instrument rather than of the practice: **C16001 counts Mandarin and Cantonese together.** The Census publishes a single Chinese column, although the two languages are not mutually intelligible when they are spoken aloud.

On paper the distinction hardly matters, since written Chinese serves speakers of either, which is precisely why a translated form represents such unusually good value. On the telephone it matters completely, because a patient who calls and reaches somebody speaking the other language has had a worse experience than if nobody had answered in Chinese at all — the practice having advertised a capability it turned out not to possess.

What a practice can honestly publish, therefore, is not the phrase "Chinese spoken" but a statement of which spoken languages the front desk genuinely offers and during which hours it offers them. A qualified line reading "Mandarin, weekday mornings" brings you patients you are equipped to serve, whereas an unqualified promise of Chinese brings you a telephone call that ends badly — and the patient on the other end of it tells people.

A practice that is bilingual only until lunch loses calls it never hears about, which is the hardest kind of loss to notice: there is no voicemail, no missed-call log entry that looks different from any other, and no complaint. The appointment simply happens somewhere else.

## Written language: two audiences, not one with a different font

Where you do translate, Simplified and Traditional are genuinely different audiences with different vocabulary preferences rather than the same text in another typeface.

Simplified suits patients more likely to be of mainland background, which is common in Rowland Heights, Hacienda Heights and parts of San Gabriel. Traditional suits those more likely to be of Taiwan background, historically more common in Monterey Park, Arcadia and San Marino. Choosing wrongly does not merely fail to help — a page in the unfamiliar script reads as careless to precisely the people it was written for.

Settle the question by asking your own patients rather than by inferring it from a city average, since your records describe the people who actually walk through the door while the Census describes everybody who happens to live nearby, and those are different populations.

## The forms, the reminders, and the order to do this in

The work that pays first is rarely the marketing:

* **Intake and consent forms** in the patient's written language, completed on their own phone before they arrive. That moves the translation out of the waiting room, where it currently gets done aloud by whichever relative came along.
* **Appointment reminders** in the language each patient prefers, which is a setting in most modern systems rather than a project.
* **After-visit instructions** — how a medication is taken, when to return. This is the one where getting it wrong has a clinical consequence rather than a commercial one.
* **A Google Business Profile** whose description, posts and answers exist in Chinese, because a great deal of local searching happens in Chinese even when the searcher is perfectly bilingual. People default to their first language for local search.

Automatic translation on your website communicates something before a visitor has read a word of your content: this practice did not think about me. It also mistranslates clinical and administrative terms reliably, and "consultation," "estimate" and "deposit" each carry specific meanings that a machine flattens.

## Signage, and the same logic online

Storefront signage across the valley runs from Chinese-only to English-only, and the choice signals who you are for before anybody reads a word. Chinese characters crammed small beneath a dominant English logo read, correctly, as not quite meant. Equal visual weight — same size, same placement, neither language stacked as a subtitle — reads as an invitation.

The same logic applies online, where an English homepage paired with a Chinese page buried three clicks deep in a smaller font is simply the digital version of that undersized sign. None of this demands a large budget; what it demands is deciding that this audience is worth building for deliberately, and then beginning with the telephone and the paperwork rather than with the brochure.
