> **TL;DR** — Verify certification yourself on the government's public product list rather than accepting a salesperson's description of it. Insist on running your own five most common visit types in a sandbox, because a scripted demo tells you nothing about the workflow you will repeat forty times a day. And establish before signing what a complete export of your data costs and what format it arrives in, since that single answer determines whether this is a decision or a marriage.

Selecting an electronic health record is the largest software decision most small practices ever make, and it is usually made under conditions almost perfectly designed to produce a poor one: a scripted demonstration, a feature comparison chart supplied by the vendor, and a deadline attached to a discount.

The good news is that a handful of questions do most of the predictive work, and none of them appear on the comparison chart.

## Verify certification yourself

Vendors describe their certification status in marketing language, and certification is in fact a matter of public record that you can check in a few minutes without asking anybody.

Health IT certification under the federal program means a product has been tested against specified criteria by an accredited body, and certified products are listed publicly on the Certified Health IT Product List. Look the product up, confirm the version you are actually being sold is the version listed, and note what it is certified for, since certification is granted against particular criteria rather than as a general seal of quality.

This matters practically because certification interacts with program participation and reporting obligations, and discovering a mismatch after implementation is expensive in a way that checking a public database is not.

## Make them let you drive

A demonstration is a performance, rehearsed by somebody who knows exactly which path avoids the awkward screens, and watching one tells you almost nothing about what your Tuesday will feel like.

Ask for sandbox access and run your own five most common visit types end to end: the routine follow-up, the new patient, the one with three chronic conditions, the quick medication question, and whatever your specialty's awkward case is. Count the clicks. A workflow that costs three extra clicks is not an inconvenience when you repeat it forty times a day; it is roughly an hour, every day, permanently.

Have the person who will actually use it do this. The physician's opinion and the medical assistant's opinion diverge sharply on most systems, and the practice will live with whichever one was not consulted.

## Ask what leaving costs, before joining

This is the question that separates a considered decision from a trap, and it is the one vendors are least comfortable answering in writing.

Ask precisely: if we leave in three years, what does a complete export of our data cost, what format does it arrive in, and does it include the clinical notes, the documents, the images and the schedule history, or only some of those? A structured export you can actually import elsewhere is a different object from a stack of PDFs, and both are described as "your data" in a sales conversation.

Get the answer in writing before signing, because the negotiating position never improves later. A vendor who answers plainly is telling you they expect to keep you by being good; one who becomes vague is telling you something too.

## The costs that are not on the quotation

The monthly per-provider figure is the number everybody compares, and it is regularly the smaller half of the real total.

Implementation and data migration from your current system are frequently charged separately and are frequently substantial. Training is usually billed and always costs more in lost clinic time than in fees. Interfaces to a lab, an imaging center or a billing clearinghouse are commonly individually priced. Support may be tiered, so the responsive support is an upgrade rather than the default.

Ask for a three-year total including all of the above, not a monthly figure. Vendors can produce this and mostly do not volunteer it.

## What matters less than the sales process suggests

Feature counts are close to meaningless, because every system in the category has hundreds of features and you will use a few dozen.

The specialty-specific templates that feature heavily in demonstrations are worth less than they appear, since most practices modify them within a month anyway. Artificial intelligence features are worth evaluating on whether they save your specific documentation time rather than on whether they exist. And the interface's appearance genuinely does not matter beyond legibility, which is fortunate, as the good systems in this category are rarely the handsome ones.

## The honest recommendation

For most small practices the correct answer is the boring one: a widely used certified system with a large installed base in your specialty, bought at a negotiated three-year total, with the exit terms in writing.

The cheapest EHR is generally the one you do not switch away from, which is an argument for spending real effort on this decision once rather than repeating it. It is also an argument against switching for marginal gains, and that is the subject of a separate article.
