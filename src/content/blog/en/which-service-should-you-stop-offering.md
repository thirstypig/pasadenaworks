> **TL;DR** — The service to cut is usually the one that eats the most hours relative to what it pays, causes the most scheduling headaches, and that you'd feel relief walking away from. Run the math on time-per-dollar for everything you offer, not just your gut feeling. If a service is losing money and the only reason you keep it is "customers ask for it," that's not a reason — it's a habit.

The service you should stop offering is the one you'd hesitate to name out loud, because some part of you already knows. Most owners can guess it in about ten seconds if they're honest. The hard part isn't identifying it. It's admitting you built a business that includes something you should quit.

Here's how that happens. You start a business and say yes to everything, because you need the revenue and you don't yet have the standing to say no. A client asks if you also do X. You don't do X, but you figure it out, because turning down money when you're new feels reckless. Three years later X is a line item on your website, and you've never gone back and asked whether it should be.

## Do the time math, not the feelings math

Most owners know roughly what they charge for each service. Almost none of them track how long each one actually takes, including the parts that don't get billed — the back-and-forth emails, the reschedules, the troubleshooting after the fact. That gap is where a bad service line hides.

Pull your last 10-15 jobs for each service you offer. For each one, write down what you were paid and roughly how many hours it took start to finish, including admin. Divide. You'll get an hourly number per service, and it is very common for one of them to be embarrassingly low — sometimes below what you'd pay someone else to do it.

This is just the 80/20 rule applied to your own client list instead of a textbook: a small share of what you do usually produces most of what you keep. The other side of the split isn't neutral. It's not "smaller but fine" — it's often actively costing you, once you count the hours nobody's paying for.

If a service pencils out at $15/hour once you count everything, you're not offering a service. You're subsidizing it with money from the services that pay properly.

## Ask these four questions about every offering

Once the math points at a candidate, check it against these before you commit:

**Which one takes the most time relative to what it earns?** You just calculated this. Write the number down somewhere you'll actually look at again, not just in your head.

**Which one causes the most scheduling and logistics pain?** Some services are profitable on paper but wreck your calendar — they require odd hours, extra equipment, coordination with other vendors, or last-minute changes that ripple into everything else you had planned that day. That cost doesn't show up on an invoice, but it shows up in how tired you are and how many other jobs you turn down to protect the room for this one.

**Which one would you be relieved to stop doing?** This is the gut-check question, and it's not soft — owners are usually right about this before they can articulate why. If you dread a certain type of job before it's even on the calendar, your body has already run the analysis your spreadsheet is about to confirm.

**Which one no longer fits who you want to be as a business?** Businesses evolve. The handyman work you took on to survive year one might not match the higher-end remodeling reputation you've since built. Keeping it can actively confuse potential customers about what you actually do — which hurts the referrals and reviews for the work you actually want.

If a service fails two or more of these, that's your answer.

## "But customers ask for it" isn't a reason

This is the objection that keeps unprofitable services alive for years, and it doesn't hold up. Customers ask for lots of things. That's not the same as it being worth your time to provide them.

If a service loses money, causes headaches, and you'd be relieved to drop it, "someone might want it" doesn't change any of that math. It just means you'll need a short, plain answer when they ask — "we don't do that anymore, but here's who does" is a fine sentence, and most customers respect a business that knows what it's good at more than one that says yes to everyone.

Dropping a service isn't shrinking your business. It's telling the truth about what your business already is, so the parts that are actually working can get more of your attention. That's usually the real reason growth stalls — not too few offerings, but too many mediocre ones crowding out the good one.
