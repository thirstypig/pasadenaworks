> **TL;DR** — The service to eliminate is ordinarily the one consuming the most hours relative to what it pays, generating the most scheduling difficulty, and which you would feel genuine relief walking away from. Run the arithmetic on time-per-dollar across everything you offer, rather than relying on instinct alone. If a service is losing money and the sole reason you retain it is that customers ask for it, that does not constitute a reason: it constitutes a habit.

The service you ought to stop offering is the one you would hesitate to name aloud, because some portion of you already knows which it is. Most owners can identify it within roughly ten seconds provided they are honest with themselves. The genuinely difficult part is not the identification itself but admitting that you have constructed a business incorporating something you ought to abandon.

Here is how that situation ordinarily arises: you establish a business and consent to everything, because you require the revenue and do not yet possess the standing to decline. A client inquires whether you also perform X, and although you do not perform X, you work it out anyway, because refusing money when you are new feels reckless. Three years afterward X has become a line item on your website, and you have never returned to ask whether it belongs there.

## Do the time math, not the feelings math

Most owners know approximately what they charge for each individual service. Almost none of them track how long each one genuinely consumes, including the portions that never get billed — the back-and-forth emails, the reschedules, the troubleshooting afterward. That discrepancy is precisely the place where an unprofitable service line conceals itself indefinitely.

Retrieve your last ten to fifteen jobs for each service you offer. For each one, record what you were paid and approximately how many hours it consumed from beginning to end, administration included. Divide the first figure by the second, and you will obtain an effective hourly rate for each service, and it is exceedingly common for one of them to prove embarrassingly low — occasionally beneath what you would pay somebody else to perform it.

This amounts to the 80/20 rule applied to your own client list rather than to a textbook: a small proportion of what you do ordinarily produces most of what you retain. The other side of that split is not neutral, and it is not merely smaller but acceptable: frequently it is costing you actively, once you have counted the hours nobody is paying for.

If a service works out at fifteen dollars an hour once everything is counted, you are not offering a service at all. You are subsidizing it with money earned by the services that pay properly.

## Ask these four questions about every offering

Once the arithmetic has identified a candidate, examine it against the following before committing:

**Which particular offering consumes the most time relative to what it actually earns?** You have just calculated this, so record the resulting number somewhere you will genuinely consult it again, rather than retaining it approximately in your head.

**Which particular offering generates the most scheduling and logistical difficulty?** Certain services are profitable on paper yet wreck your calendar, because they demand unusual hours, additional equipment, coordination with other vendors, or last-minute alterations that ripple through everything else planned for that day. That cost never appears on an invoice, although it appears reliably in how exhausted you are and in how many other jobs you decline in order to preserve room for this one.

**Which particular offering would you feel genuinely relieved to stop performing?** This constitutes the instinctive question, and it is emphatically not a soft one, because owners are usually correct about this well before they can articulate why. If you dread a particular category of job before it has even reached the calendar, your body has already performed the analysis your spreadsheet is about to confirm.

**Which one no longer fits who you want to be as a business?** Businesses evolve continuously, and the handyman work you accepted in order to survive your first year may no longer match the higher-end remodeling reputation you have since constructed. Retaining it can actively confuse prospective customers about what you actually do, which damages the referrals and reviews attached to the work you genuinely want.

If a particular service fails two or more of these criteria, you effectively have your answer.

## "But customers ask for it" isn't a reason

This is the objection that sustains unprofitable services for years at a time, and it does not withstand any serious examination. Customers request a great many things, which is not remotely equivalent to those things being worth your time to provide.

If a service loses money, generates difficulty, and you would feel relieved to drop it, then the possibility that somebody might want it alters none of that arithmetic. It merely means you will require a short, plain answer when they ask. "We don't do that anymore, but here's who does" is a perfectly adequate sentence, and most customers respect a business that knows what it is good at considerably more than one that consents to everything.

Dropping a service does not constitute shrinking your business; it constitutes telling the truth about what your business already is, so that the parts genuinely working can receive more of your attention. That is ordinarily the real reason growth stalls — not too few offerings, but too many mediocre ones crowding out the good one.
