> **TL;DR** — For most small businesses, a real website runs $1,500–$6,000 as a one-time build from a freelancer or small shop, or $0–$30/month if you build it yourself on a DIY platform like Squarespace or Wix. Agencies charge more for the same result. AI website builders and "vibe coding" tools can get you a live page for almost nothing up front — but cheap to launch and cheap over three years aren't the same question, and that one's still genuinely unsettled.

If someone quotes you $300 for a website, or $30,000, both numbers can be correct — they're just answering different questions. Here's what actually drives the price, and where AI tools fit into that math.

## What actually moves the price

Three things drive most of the cost difference:

* **Custom vs. template.** A templated site (DIY builder, or a freelancer using a theme) is cheaper because nobody's building your layout from scratch. A fully custom design costs more because someone is.
* **Who writes the words.** Real copywriting — someone actually asking what makes your business different and writing that down — takes real time. A lot of "expensive" website quotes are really copywriting quotes wearing a web design hat.
* **What it connects to.** A site that just displays information is simpler than one wired into a booking calendar, a CRM, or online ordering. Every integration is scope, and scope is money.

## What you actually get at each price point

**$0–$30/month, DIY (Squarespace, Wix, Google Business Profile alone).** Fine for a business that mainly needs to exist online and be findable — hours, location, phone number, a few photos. You're doing the setup yourself, on a template.

**$1,500–$6,000, one-time, freelancer or small shop.** A real site built for your business specifically: your words, your structure, mobile-checked, set up so Google can actually read it. This is the range most small businesses that plan to be found on search should be looking at.

**$10,000+, agency.** You're paying for a team, a process, and account management — not necessarily a better end result than the range above. Sometimes worth it if you need ongoing strategy alongside the build. Often you're paying for overhead.

## What about AI website builders and "vibe coding"?

This is the part that's changed the most in the last year, and it's also the part where honest advice has to include "we don't fully know yet."

The upside is real: AI tools can generate a working, published website with real pages and a working contact form in minutes, for close to nothing. For a business that just needs *something live today*, that's a genuine option, not a gimmick.

The catch shows up later, and it shows up in a few specific ways. Security reviews of AI-generated code have found vulnerabilities at a meaningfully higher rate than hand-written code. Sites built this way tend to accumulate messy, hard-to-fix structure faster than a site someone actually planned — which matters the day you want to add something new and it turns out the foundation can't easily support it. And a few businesses have gotten burned by AI-generated legal boilerplate — a cookie notice or privacy page that was invented rather than actually accurate to what the site does, which is its own kind of liability.

None of that means AI-built sites are a bad idea. It means "AI website builder" and "$6,000 custom site" aren't really competing for the same customer. One is optimized for getting something live fast and cheap today. The other is optimized for holding up as your business grows, needs SEO to actually work, and needs someone to fix something six months from now without starting over. Which one is right for you depends on which of those problems you actually have.

If your website is a brochure — hours, contact info, a few photos — an AI builder or a DIY platform is a completely reasonable place to start. If your website is meant to be how people *find* you and decide to call, the SEO groundwork and the writing tend to matter more than they get credit for, and that's where paying a person still earns its cost.

## A simple way to pick a number

Ask what the website actually needs to do. If the honest answer is "exist and look legitimate," budget for the DIY end. If the honest answer is "bring in customers who are actively searching for what I do," budget for the custom-build range — and ask whoever you hire how they think about search, not just design, before you sign anything.
