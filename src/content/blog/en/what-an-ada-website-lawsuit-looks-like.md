> **TL;DR** — Plaintiffs filed 3,117 website accessibility lawsuits in federal court in 2025, up 27% on 2024, with New York, Florida and Illinois leading. California federal courts saw four. That is not because California businesses are safe, but because California appellate courts have held that online-only businesses are not covered by the ADA, and because the pressure here arrives as demand letters and state-court claims instead. If you run a business in the San Gabriel Valley, the second sentence matters far more than the first.

None of these articles describe our own clients, because this is a small consultancy with no published client work and inventing a case study would be worse than not writing one. What follows is read from public filing records and the firms that count them.

## The national picture, with numbers

The annual counts are published, and they move around more than the coverage suggests.

Plaintiffs filed **3,117** website accessibility lawsuits in federal court in 2025, against **2,452** in 2024 — a 27% increase that, as the firm tracking these notes, almost exactly reverses the declines of the two preceding years. The leading venues were New York with **1,021**, Florida with **961** and Illinois with **585**.

So the trend is neither the runaway explosion nor the quiet collapse that different vendors will describe to you depending on what they are selling. The volume goes up and down from year to year, and it remains concentrated in a small number of jurisdictions.

## Why California's number is four

This is the part worth understanding properly, because the surface reading of it is dangerously wrong.

California federal courts saw **four** website accessibility lawsuits in 2025, and the explanation is not that California is a soft jurisdiction. It is that both federal and state appellate courts in California have concluded that **online-only businesses are not covered by the ADA**, which removes the federal route for a large class of defendants — and that the activity has moved to demand letters and to state court instead.

That distinction matters enormously for a local business, and A dentist, a restaurant or a shop in Alhambra is not an online-only business; it is a physical place with a website, which is a different posture entirely from a purely online seller. And the mechanism that reaches it is unlikely to be a federal complaint.

## What actually arrives

The realistic sequence for a small California business is not a courthouse, and it is a letter.

A demand letter names a handful of specific barriers — images without alternative text, a form whose fields have no labels, a menu that keyboard focus cannot reach — and proposes a settlement figure that is calibrated to be less than the cost of engaging a lawyer. That calibration is the point, and it is designed so that paying is the rational choice even for a business that would probably win.

The barriers cited tend to be the same short list every time, and it is the same list any competent review would produce in an afternoon. That is the useful signal buried in all of this: the things being sued over are the things that are cheapest to fix.

## What the filings do not say

Reading the record also corrects two things that vendors routinely assert to worried business owners.

There is no federal certification you can hold up, and the Department of Justice states plainly that it "does not have a regulation setting out detailed standards" and that businesses "can currently choose how they will ensure" their online offerings are accessible, describing WCAG as "helpful guidance". Nobody is in a position to certify you against a standard that has never been adopted in the first place.

And nothing in the record suggests a business becomes immune by buying a product, and what reduces exposure is the unglamorous work being done and documented.

## What to take from it

Fix the short list, and write down when you did it, and alternative text, color contrast, keyboard operation, form labels, captions — that is most of what gets cited. Every item on it is an edit to an existing page rather than a rebuild of anything.

Keep a dated record of the work, because if a letter ever arrives, the difference between a business that can show a remediation history and one that cannot is the difference between a conversation and a check.

And read anything sold to you against the numbers above, and A vendor citing a nationwide filing count to a San Gabriel Valley restaurant is quoting a statistic from the wrong forum, and now you know it.
