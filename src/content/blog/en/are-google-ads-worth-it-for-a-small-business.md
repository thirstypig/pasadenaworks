> **TL;DR** — Google Ads is worth it if you sell something with decent margin, the person searching is ready to buy now, and someone actually checks the account every week. It's not worth it if you're hoping a few hundred dollars will "try out" advertising, because that's not enough money to learn anything before it runs out.

Yes, for the right business. No, for a lot of businesses that try it. The difference isn't luck — it's whether your numbers work and whether anyone is minding the store.

Here's the math nobody explains before you hand Google your credit card.

## What clicks actually cost right now

Cost per click varies wildly by industry, and the gap matters more than any average. Across all industries, the average cost per click in 2026 sits around $5.42, but that number hides the real story. Attorneys pay close to $9.87 a click. Home improvement contractors pay around $8.33. Dentists pay about $8.00. On the cheap end, arts and entertainment businesses pay as little as $1.63, and restaurants around $2.05.

Why the spread? Google Ads is an auction, and the price tracks how much a new customer is worth to the businesses bidding. A personal injury lawyer can afford $10 a click because one case is worth tens of thousands of dollars. A restaurant can't, because a $10-click for a $15 lunch order doesn't pencil out.

That's the first gut check: figure out what a click costs in your category, then figure out what a customer is actually worth to you — not what you wish they were worth. If you're a plumber charging $150 for an average service call and it costs $8 a click with maybe 1 in 20 clicks turning into a booked job, that's $160 to land one customer. If that job clears $150 in profit, you're losing money before you've paid for the truck or the labor. If it's a $2,000 water heater replacement, the math flips completely.

## What a real budget looks like

A lot of small businesses set aside $200 or $300 a month, run ads for three weeks, see nothing, and conclude "Google Ads doesn't work." That's not a fair test. It's not enough money to get enough clicks to know anything.

Realistic starting budgets for a small local business run more like $1,000 to $2,500 a month, and a genuinely competitive category — home services in a big metro, personal injury law, anything with a wide service area — can need $3,000 to $5,000 a month before the data is reliable enough to optimize against. Below roughly $10–15 a day, you're not really running a campaign — you're buying a handful of clicks a week and hoping.

Do the arithmetic before you commit: take your industry's average CPC, decide how many clicks you can afford to test with in a month, and multiply. If that number is nowhere near $500, either your budget needs to grow or your keywords need to get a lot narrower — a specific service in a specific neighborhood, not "plumber" statewide.

## When it pays off, and when it's a waste

Google Ads works when three things are true at once:

- **The person searching is ready to act now.** "Emergency AC repair Pasadena" is a buyer. "How does air conditioning work" is not. Google Ads is built for intent-driven searches — someone already decided they need what you sell and is looking for who sells it. It's a poor fit for building awareness of something people don't yet know they want.
- **The margin per customer covers the cost of finding them, with room left over.** A $50 service with a $6 CPC and a 5% conversion rate means it costs $120 to land a $50 sale. No amount of optimizing fixes that ratio — the business itself doesn't have room for paid acquisition at that price point, and no agency should tell you otherwise.
- **Someone is actually watching the account.** Google Ads doesn't run itself well. Left alone, campaigns drift toward broad, expensive, badly-targeted traffic because the platform's default settings favor spending your budget, not spending it well. A campaign nobody checks for a month is a campaign quietly bleeding money on searches that were never going to convert.

Where it's a bad idea: thin-margin businesses, one-time low-dollar purchases, anyone expecting a payoff in the first two weeks, and anyone who won't look at the account after it launches. If any of those describe your situation, that money is usually better spent on a Google Business Profile that's actually filled out, or a website that ranks for what people search organically — slower, but it doesn't stop working the day you stop paying.

The honest answer for most small businesses in Pasadena and the San Gabriel Valley: if you sell a service worth $200 or more per customer, have $1,000+ a month to actually test with, and someone will check the account weekly for the first two months, it's worth trying. If any of those three aren't true, fix that first.
