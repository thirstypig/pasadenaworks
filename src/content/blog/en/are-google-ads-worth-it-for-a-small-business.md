> **TL;DR** — Google Ads is worth it provided you sell something carrying decent margin, the person searching is ready to buy immediately, and somebody actually reviews the account every week. It is not worth it if you are hoping that a few hundred dollars will "try out" advertising, because that is not sufficient expenditure to learn anything whatsoever before it is exhausted.

Yes, for the appropriate business; no, for a great many businesses that attempt it regardless. The difference is not luck but whether your numbers work and whether anybody is minding the store, and what follows is the arithmetic nobody explains to you before you surrender your credit card to Google.

## What clicks actually cost right now

Cost per click varies enormously by industry, and the gap matters considerably more than any average does. According to [WordStream's 2026 Google Ads benchmarks](https://www.wordstream.com/blog/2026-google-ads-benchmarks), the average cost per click across all industries sits at $5.42, but that figure conceals the actual story. Attorneys and legal services pay $9.87 per click, home improvement contractors around $8.33, and dentists $8.00. At the inexpensive end, arts and entertainment businesses pay as little as $1.63, and restaurants $2.05 — a near-sixfold spread from one end of the range to the other.

The reason for that spread is that Google Ads operates as an auction, and the price tracks how much a new customer is worth to the businesses bidding. A personal injury lawyer can afford ten dollars per click because a single case is worth tens of thousands, whereas a restaurant cannot, because a ten-dollar click against a fifteen-dollar lunch order does not pencil out.

The same benchmarks make the point even more sharply at the level of an acquired lead rather than a click: attorneys and legal services run **$131.63 per lead**, while restaurants run **$30.57**. That is the number to compare against your own margin, because a click is not a customer.

That constitutes the first gut check: establish what a click costs within your particular category, and subsequently establish what an acquired customer is genuinely worth to you, rather than what you wish they were worth. If you are a plumber charging $150 for an average service call, and clicks cost $8 with perhaps one in twenty converting into a booked job, then landing one customer costs $160. If that job clears $150 in profit, you are losing money before paying for the truck or the labor. If instead the engagement is a $2,000 water heater replacement, the arithmetic reverses entirely.

## What a real budget looks like

A great many small businesses set aside $200 or $300 monthly, run advertisements for three weeks, observe nothing, and conclude that Google Ads does not work. That does not constitute an equitable assessment, because it is insufficient expenditure to accumulate sufficient clicks to establish anything whatsoever.

Realistic starting budgets for a small local business run closer to $1,000 to $2,500 monthly, and a genuinely competitive category — home services in a large metropolitan area, personal injury law, anything carrying a wide service area — can require $3,000 to $5,000 monthly before the data becomes reliable enough to optimize against. Below roughly $10 to $15 daily you are not really running a campaign at all; you are purchasing a handful of clicks each week and hoping.

Perform the arithmetic before committing: take the average cost per click within your industry, decide how many clicks you can afford to test with in a month, and multiply the two. If the resulting number is nowhere near $500, then either your budget needs to grow or your keywords need to become considerably narrower — a specific service within a specific neighborhood, rather than "plumber" across the entire state.

## When it pays off, and when it's a waste

Google Ads works where three conditions hold simultaneously:

* **The person searching is ready to act now.** "Emergency AC repair Pasadena" is a buyer, whereas "how does air conditioning work" is not. Google Ads is constructed for intent-driven searches, where somebody has already determined they need what you sell and is establishing who sells it. It is a poor instrument for building awareness of something people do not yet know they want.
* **The margin per customer covers the cost of finding them, with room remaining.** A $50 service against a $6 cost per click and a 5% conversion rate means spending $120 to land a $50 sale. No quantity of optimizing repairs that ratio, because the business itself lacks room for paid acquisition at that price point, and no agency should tell you otherwise.
* **Somebody is genuinely supervising the account.** Google Ads does not administer itself competently, and campaigns left unattended drift toward broad, expensive, poorly targeted traffic, because the platform's default settings favor spending your budget rather than spending it carefully. A campaign nobody has examined for a month is a campaign quietly hemorrhaging expenditure against searches that were never going to convert.

Where it constitutes a bad idea: thin-margin businesses, one-time low-value purchases, anybody expecting a payoff within the first fortnight, and anybody who will not examine the account after it launches. If any of those describe your situation, that money is usually better directed toward a Google Business Profile that is genuinely filled out, or a website ranking for what people search organically — slower, certainly, although it does not cease functioning on the day you discontinue payment.

The honest answer for most small businesses in Pasadena and the San Gabriel Valley: if you sell a service worth $200 or more per customer, have $1,000 or more monthly to genuinely test with, and somebody will examine the account weekly for the first two months, then it is worth attempting. If any of those three conditions is not satisfied, remedy that particular one beforehand.
