> **TL;DR** — Find out which registrar holds it and when it expires, because everything else depends on those two facts. If somebody cooperative currently holds it, a transfer amounts to ordinary administrative paperwork. If it has expired, there is a recovery window before it returns to general availability, and that window is shorter than most people assume. If somebody uncooperative holds it, the routes are slow and none of them is a button you can press.

Losing a domain is not remotely like losing a password, because a domain is a registration held by a particular party at a registrar, and getting one back means either persuading that party, persuading the registrar, or waiting for the registration to lapse. There is no support line that simply restores it to you because you are the business it names.

That is the unwelcome half of the picture, and the useful half is that most actual cases turn out considerably more ordinary than the fear surrounding them suggests.

## Establish two facts first

Before deciding anything, look the domain up in a public WHOIS or ICANN lookup and write down two things: **which registrar holds it** and **when it expires**.

The registrar matters because it determines who you are actually dealing with, and it is frequently not who you assumed. Plenty of businesses discover the domain is at a registrar they have an account with already, or one their previous supplier resold from, which changes the conversation entirely.

The expiry date matters because it converts an open-ended problem into a dated one. It also tells you whether you are in the ordinary case or the urgent one.

## If somebody cooperative holds it

This is by some distance the common case, and it is very largely administrative rather than adversarial.

Inter-registrar transfers are governed by ICANN's published transfer policy, and the mechanics are the same everywhere: the current holder unlocks the domain and provides an authorisation code, you begin a transfer at the registrar you want it to live at, and it completes over several days. If you would rather not move registrars at all, most registrars also support changing the registrant on the existing registration, which is simpler.

Two things about this reliably surprise people, and both are worth knowing in advance. Transfers are commonly locked for a period after a registration is created or after registrant details change, so a domain that was just moved may not be movable again immediately. And the process is deliberately slow, because the slowness is a security feature protecting against exactly the hijacking you would otherwise be reading about.

Start the process well before the renewal date, since a transfer running against a deadline is a considerably more stressful transfer than one with several weeks in hand.

## If it has already expired

Expiry is not instantaneous loss, and the timeline is worth knowing before you need it.

A lapsed domain typically passes through a grace period during which the original holder can still renew it, often at the ordinary price, followed by a redemption period where recovery is still possible but carries a substantial fee, and finally deletion, after which it returns to general availability and anybody may register it.

The practical recommendation is straightforward: act within the earliest recovery window available to you. The cost rises sharply at each stage, and the last stage is not a stage at all — once a domain is publicly available, it may be registered by somebody who watches expiring names for resale, and at that point you are negotiating a purchase rather than a recovery.

## If somebody uncooperative holds it

Here the honest answer is that your options are slow, and it is better to know that than to be told otherwise.

Where a registrar has refused a transfer improperly, ICANN operates a complaint process, and that is the correct route rather than repeated arguments with support staff. Where a domain matching a trademark has been registered in bad faith, there are formal dispute procedures, and they are real but they involve cost, evidence, and months rather than days.

Where somebody is holding your domain over a disputed invoice, the fastest resolution is very often to settle the invoice, even when you believe it is wrong, and then move the domain somewhere you control. That is admittedly unsatisfying advice, and it is nonetheless frequently cheaper than the alternative, concluding with you in possession of the asset rather than merely vindicated about it.

## When to stop trying

Sometimes the correct decision is to let it go, and it is worth saying so plainly, because sunk cost keeps businesses fighting for names long past the point of sense.

A domain is worth the traffic, the links and the email that depend on it. If the site was new, if almost nobody links to it, and if the address was never printed on anything, a different domain and a redirect from wherever you can manage costs far less than a dispute. If the domain has ten years of accumulated links, printed signage and an email address every customer has, it is worth considerable effort.

Establish which of those two situations you are actually in before committing yourself to the fight.
