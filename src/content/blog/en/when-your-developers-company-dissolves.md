> **TL;DR** — Nothing breaks on the day a supplier closes, which is what makes this dangerous. The site keeps serving and the email keeps arriving until a payment fails or a renewal lapses, both of which happen on a schedule nobody is watching. Establish within the week which registrar holds the domain, when it expires, and whether you can log in — because a domain that lapses and completes its expiry cycle returns to general availability, and at that point you are negotiating a purchase rather than recovering an asset.

A supplier closing is different from a supplier going quiet, and it is worse in one specific respect: there is often nobody left to ask, and the accounts do not know their owner has stopped trading.

## Why nothing appears to happen at first

Hosting and domains are prepaid and automated, which means the infrastructure outlives the company by exactly as long as the billing cycle.

The site keeps serving pages. Email keeps flowing. Nothing announces itself. Then a card on file expires, or an annual renewal comes due against a bank account that has been closed, and a service stops without warning — usually the domain, and usually first noticed because email stops.

The interval between the company closing and the first failure is your entire window, and it is typically weeks to months.

## The three things to establish this week

**Where is the domain, and whose is it?** A public WHOIS or ICANN lookup will name the registrar and the expiry date, and the expiry date turns this from an anxiety into a deadline. Then try to log in. If you cannot, you do not control it, whatever the paperwork implies.

**Where is the hosting, and who is paying?** Find the invoice, or the card statement line, and identify the provider. If the account is in the dissolved company's name, contact the provider directly and explain the situation — they deal with this regularly and have processes.

**What do you actually have?** Take complete copies now, while the site is still up: the pages as they render, the images, the text, and any data the site holds — enquiries, subscribers, bookings. Doing this on a working site takes an afternoon; reconstructing from an archive after it goes dark takes considerably longer and produces less.

## Why the domain is the urgent one

The others are replaceable and the domain is not, which is why it goes first regardless of what else is happening.

A lapsed domain moves through a grace period, then a redemption period carrying a substantial fee, and then deletion, after which it returns to general availability and anybody may register it — including people who monitor expiring names for resale. WIPO has administered 84,743 domain disputes since 1999 and the formal route exists, but it addresses bad-faith registrations of trademark-matching names, not the ordinary case of a name that simply lapsed and was picked up.

So the cheap moment is now, and the expensive moment is a few months from now.

## Dealing with a registrar when the registrant no longer exists

This is the awkward scenario, and it is more tractable than it sounds.

Registrars have procedures for a registrant that has dissolved, and they are documentary: evidence of the business relationship, invoices showing you paid for the domain, correspondence, your own business registration. Approach the registrar directly rather than trying to find a former director, and expect it to be slow and form-driven rather than a conversation.

Start before the expiry date, not after. Everything about this gets harder once the name has lapsed.

## What to put in place afterwards

Once you have control, spend the afternoon that prevents the repeat.

Register the domain in the business's name, on a card the business controls, with auto-renew on and a contact address that is a shared business mailbox rather than one person's. Consider registering several years forward, which removes the annual opportunity for this to happen. Own the hosting account and add whoever does the work as a user. And write down, somewhere findable, which accounts exist and where.

That list is the thing most businesses do not have, and it is the difference between an afternoon of admin and a season of reconstruction.
