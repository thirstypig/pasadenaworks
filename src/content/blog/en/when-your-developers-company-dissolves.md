> **TL;DR** — Nothing breaks on the day a supplier closes, which is precisely what makes the situation dangerous: the site keeps serving and the email keeps arriving until a payment fails or a renewal lapses, both of which happen on a schedule nobody is monitoring. Establish within the week which registrar holds the domain, when it expires, and whether you can log in — because a domain that lapses and completes its expiry cycle returns to general availability, and at that point you are negotiating a purchase rather than recovering an asset.

A supplier closing is different from a supplier going quiet, and it is worse in one specific respect: there is frequently nobody remaining to ask, and the accounts themselves have no idea their owner has stopped trading.

## Why nothing appears to happen at first

Hosting and domains are prepaid and automated, which means the infrastructure outlives the company by exactly as long as the billing cycle happens to run.

The site keeps serving pages and email keeps flowing, nothing announces itself whatsoever, and then a card on file expires, or an annual renewal comes due against a bank account that has already been closed, and a service stops without warning — usually the domain, and usually noticed first because email stops arriving.

The interval between the company closing and the first failure constitutes your entire window, and it typically measures somewhere between weeks and months.

## The three things to establish this week

**Where is the domain, and whose is it?** A public WHOIS or ICANN lookup will identify the registrar and the expiry date, and that expiry date converts this from an anxiety into a deadline. Then attempt to log in, because if you cannot, you do not control it, whatever the paperwork implies.

**Where is the hosting, and who is paying?** Locate the invoice, or alternatively the card statement line, and identify the provider; then, if the account is registered in the dissolved company's name, contact the provider directly and explain the situation, because they encounter this regularly and have established processes.

**What do you actually have?** Take complete copies now, while the site is still up: the pages as they render, the images, the text, and whatever data the site holds — enquiries, subscribers, bookings. Doing this on a working site takes an afternoon, whereas reconstructing from an archive after it goes dark takes considerably longer and produces considerably less.

## Why the domain is the urgent one

The others are replaceable and the domain is not, which is why it goes first regardless of whatever else is happening.

A lapsed domain moves through a grace period, then a redemption period carrying a substantial fee, and then deletion, after which it returns to general availability and anybody at all may register it — including people who monitor expiring names for resale. WIPO has administered 84,743 domain disputes since 1999 and the formal route certainly exists, but it addresses bad-faith registrations of trademark-matching names rather than the ordinary case of a name that simply lapsed and was picked up.

So the inexpensive moment is now, and the expensive moment arrives a few months from now.

## Dealing with a registrar when the registrant no longer exists

This is the genuinely awkward scenario, and it is considerably more tractable than it initially sounds.

Registrars maintain procedures for a registrant that has dissolved, and those procedures are documentary: evidence of the business relationship, invoices demonstrating that you paid for the domain, correspondence, your own business registration. Approach the registrar directly rather than attempting to locate a former director, and expect the process to be slow and form-driven rather than conversational.

Start before the expiry date rather than afterwards, because everything about this becomes harder once the name has lapsed.

## What to put in place afterwards

Once you have recovered control, spend the single afternoon that prevents this entire situation from repeating.

Register the domain in the business's name, on a card the business controls, with auto-renew enabled and a contact address that is a shared business mailbox rather than one person's. Consider registering several years forward, which eliminates the annual opportunity for this to happen again. Own the hosting account yourself and add whoever does the work as an additional user, and write down, somewhere genuinely findable, exactly which accounts exist and where they live.

That list is the thing most businesses simply do not have, and it constitutes the difference between an afternoon of administration and an entire season of reconstruction.
