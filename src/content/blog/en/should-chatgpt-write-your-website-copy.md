> **TL;DR** — Google's own position is that AI-written content is not penalized for being AI-written; what gets penalized is "scaled content abuse," pages generated in bulk with no real expertise behind them. The honest risk in letting an assistant write your copy is not a ranking algorithm. It is that the copy comes out sounding like everyone else's, on a site whose whole case is that it doesn't.

The question arrives with a specific shape: you have five service pages to write, a deadline, and a chat window that will produce all five in about ninety seconds. Whether to use it is less about whether it's allowed than about what you get.

## What Google actually penalizes

Google's own guidance addresses this directly, and it draws the line somewhere more specific than "AI-generated."

"Appropriate use of AI or automation," Google writes, "is not against our guidelines." The violation is "using automation, including AI, to generate content primarily to manipulate ranking in search results," which the spam policies describe as scaled content abuse: publishing large quantities of pages with "little to no value to users," regardless of whether a human or a model typed them.

That is a narrower target than the fear usually is. A dentist's office writing its own five service pages with an assistant's help, then checking every claim, is not what the policy describes. A directory site generating two thousand city-service combinations from a template, with a model filling in the blanks, is exactly what it describes.

## What the assistant is actually good at

An assistant is a fast first draft, and a genuinely useful one for the parts of a page that are mostly structure: an opening that states what the business does, a list of services organized sensibly, a section restating what a contact form should ask for. None of that requires anything an assistant doesn't have.

What it does not have is your actual answer to "why us, and not the shop two doors down." It has never seen your storefront, doesn't know which repair you turned down last month because it wasn't worth doing right, and cannot tell a reader what your hours actually are unless you tell it first. Those specifics are exactly what separates a page that reads like a real business from one that reads like every other page an assistant has produced this week, because the model is drawing on the same patterns for all of them.

## The tell that gives it away

Pages written entirely by an assistant, unedited, tend to share a fingerprint, and it is worth learning to spot it in your own drafts before a reader does.

The sentences hedge instead of commit — "may include," "a variety of," "can help ensure" — because the model is generalizing across every business in the category rather than describing yours. The claims are vague where a real business would be specific: "years of experience" rather than a founding year, "quality service" rather than what quality actually means for the work you do. And the voice is uniformly upbeat in a way no real business writing is, because nothing this site says is ever unqualified good news, and unedited AI copy rarely admits a downside.

Every one of those is fixable in an edit pass. None of them is fixable by writing a better prompt, because the fix is information the model was never given.

## A reasonable way to use it

Draft with it, edit against it, and never publish what it wrote without a pass that adds something it couldn't have known.

Give it your actual facts first — the founding year, the specific services, the real limitations — rather than asking it to invent plausible ones, since a plausible invented fact is indistinguishable from a wrong one until a customer catches it. Ask for a structural draft, then rewrite every sentence that could describe a competitor's business as easily as yours. And read the result against this site's own voice: no "leverage," no "solutions," no claim you wouldn't be willing to defend if a customer asked you to.

The output of that process is not obviously AI-written and not obviously human-written either, because that distinction was never the one that mattered. What mattered is whether the page says something true and specific enough that only your business could have said it.
