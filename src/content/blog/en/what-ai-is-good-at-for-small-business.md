> **TL;DR** — The useful division is not between simple and complicated tasks, but between tasks where you can immediately tell whether the output is right and tasks where you cannot. An assistant is genuinely good at first drafts, at summarizing material you were never going to read, and at reformatting things. It is poor at anything requiring facts about your own business, and it is at its most dangerous when producing fluent, confident text about a subject the reader knows better than you do.

Almost every discussion of this topic operates at one of two unhelpful extremes, and a small business owner trying to decide whether any of it is worth an afternoon gets very little from either.

The dividing line that actually predicts whether a task will go well is unglamorous, and applying it takes about ten seconds per task.

## The test that sorts it

Ask yourself a single question before handing any task over to an assistant, and ask it every time: **if the answer that came back were wrong, would I notice immediately?**

If you would notice — a badly structured page, a clumsy sentence, an email in the wrong tone — then the assistant is useful, because you are the quality control and you are competent at it. If you would not notice, because the output concerns a regulation you have not read, a figure you cannot check, or a claim about your own industry that sounds plausible, then the assistant is a liability precisely in proportion to how fluent it sounds.

That is the entire framework, and everything set out below amounts to an application of it to a particular category of task.

## Where it genuinely earns its keep

**First drafts of things you find hard to start**, which covers most writing tasks a business owner postpones. The blank page is the expensive part of writing a services page, a job advertisement or an awkward email, and an assistant removes it. What comes back is rarely good, and it is considerably easier to fix something mediocre than to originate something from nothing.

**Reading things you would not otherwise have read at all**, which is where the gain is largest precisely because the baseline is zero. A supplier contract, a long policy update, a competitor's twenty-page brochure — asking for a summary and then reading the parts that summary flags is a genuine gain, because the alternative was not reading it at all. Verify anything you intend to act on.

**Reformatting and mechanical transformation**, where the task is defined tightly enough that failure is obvious. Turning notes into a structured list, converting a price sheet into a table, rewriting a paragraph three ways so you can pick one. These are tasks where correctness is immediately visible on inspection, which is precisely the condition established at the start of this article.

**Being a second opinion at an hour when nobody else is available**, which is a genuine constraint for anybody running a business alone. Asking what somebody might reasonably object to in a proposal, or what appears to be missing from a plan, produces a list that you can then work through and dismiss item by item. Some proportion of that list will be obvious enough to discard at once, and some smaller proportion will turn out to be the thing you had genuinely forgotten.

## Where it costs you money

**Anything factual about your own business**, which is the category where confident invention does the most damage. An assistant does not know your prices, your hours, your service area or which supplier you dropped last year, and it will supply plausible values for all of them if the prompt implies it should. This is the single most common way AI-written copy embarrasses a business publicly.

**Anything where the reader knows the subject better than you do**, which inverts the quality control described above. Technical copy written for a specialist audience by somebody who cannot evaluate it reads as entirely competent to its author and as obviously wrong to the audience it was written for. This is worse than clumsy honest writing by a considerable margin.

**Content produced at volume for the purpose of ranking**, where Google's own published position is worth quoting rather than paraphrasing: appropriate use of AI or automation "is not against our guidelines," and what breaches them is using automation "to generate content for the primary purpose of manipulating ranking in search results" — which its spam policies describe as scaled content abuse, producing many pages with little or no value regardless of who or what wrote them. The line being drawn is not one of authorship at all, but of whether the resulting page is actually worth somebody's time to read.

**Anything you would be unwilling to defend as your own words.** If a customer asked you to expand on a particular sentence and you found that you could not, then that sentence has no business appearing on your site under your name in the first place.

## The practical arrangement

Use it to produce a draft, and then edit against that draft rather than approving whatever it happened to produce on the first attempt.

Give it your real figures instead of asking it to invent plausible ones, since a fabricated but reasonable-sounding number is indistinguishable from a correct one until a customer finds out. Rewrite any sentence that could describe a competitor as easily as it describes you, because that sentence is where the assistant reverted to describing the category rather than your business.

And keep the tasks within the first list rather than drifting into the second, since the gains available there are real, ordinary and repeatable, which makes them considerably more valuable in practice than the gains people tend to write articles about.
