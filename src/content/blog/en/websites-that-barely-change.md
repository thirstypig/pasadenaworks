> **TL;DR** — The Internet Archive's Wayback Machine keeps dated snapshots of most public websites, which means you can look at whatever any business's site displayed in 2010 and compare it against today in roughly two minutes. Do that for a handful of long-established local businesses before you commission a redesign, because what you tend to discover is that the ones with a durable customer base altered their sites considerably less than the marketing conversation assumes, and that the elements they did alter were the practical ones.

Redesign conversations usually begin with a feeling rather than with a problem: the site looks dated, a competitor launched something, somebody pronounced the word "refresh". A useful corrective is available at no cost whatsoever, and almost nobody bothers to use it.

## Check it yourself, which takes two minutes

The Internet Archive has been capturing snapshots of public web pages since the 1990s, and its Wayback Machine presents them on a calendar.

Enter a business's address at `web.archive.org`, select a year, and open a capture; then open the live site alongside it. You are now examining the same business a decade apart, which constitutes a considerably better input to a redesign decision than a mood board does.

Do this for three or four businesses you genuinely admire — the restaurant that always has a queue, the trade supplier everybody in the valley uses, the practice that has never advertised. The point is not to imitate them but to calibrate how much visual change successful businesses actually undertake.

## What the exercise tends to show

Two patterns recur often enough to be worth naming in advance, and noticing them is the entire value of the exercise.

**Established businesses change less than the industry implies.** A site that reliably tells people what the business does, where it is and how to buy tends to survive, because there is no internal pressure whatsoever to alter something that is demonstrably already working, and the pressure to redesign therefore originates outside the business almost without exception.

**When they do change, the change is functional.** Sites acquire mobile layouts, faster loading, online ordering, accurate hours — modifications that responded to how customers were actually behaving, whereas the typography is rarely the element that moved.

That second pattern is the useful one, because it separates two things that a proposal habitually bundles together. A redesign that adds online booking constitutes a business decision, whereas a redesign that modernizes the aesthetic constitutes a preference, and preferences are entirely permissible provided they are priced as preferences rather than as necessities.

## When a redesign is genuinely the right call

None of this argues for never changing anything, and there are conditions under which a rebuild is straightforwardly correct.

If nobody can edit the site without the person who built it, that is a rebuild; if it is slow on a phone and the cause is structural, that is a rebuild; if the menus cannot be made keyboard-accessible without replacing them, that is a rebuild, and that one usually arrives alongside several other reasons. If the business genuinely does something different from whatever the site describes, then the content is wrong and no quantity of styling repairs it.

Notice that all four are functional, and that none of them amounts to "it looks like 2019".

## What to do instead, most of the time

Before commissioning anything, spend the money's worth of attention on the four questions this site keeps returning to: can somebody locate your hours, your location, your prices and a route to an actual person, on a phone, in under thirty seconds.

A site that fails those questions is worth repairing whether or not it happens to be beautiful. A site that passes them is already doing its job, and a redesign becomes a discretionary purchase, which remains a completely reasonable thing to make provided everybody involved understands that is what it is.

## The archive has a second use

While you are already inside the archive, look up your own site as well.

You will find versions of your own copy you had forgotten, claims you no longer make, prices you no longer charge, and quite possibly a page you believed was deleted. That is worth knowing for its own sake, and it occasionally answers a question about whatever you told a customer three years ago rather better than memory manages to.
