> **TL;DR** — The Internet Archive's Wayback Machine keeps dated snapshots of most public websites, which means you can look at what any business's site looked like in 2010 and compare it with today in about two minutes. Do that for a few long-established local businesses before commissioning a redesign. What you tend to find is that the ones with a durable customer base changed their sites far less than the marketing conversation assumes, and that the parts they did change were the practical ones.

Redesign conversations usually begin with a feeling rather than a problem: the site looks dated, a competitor launched something, somebody said the word "refresh". A useful corrective is available free, and almost nobody uses it.

## Check it yourself, which takes two minutes

The Internet Archive has been capturing snapshots of public web pages since the 1990s, and its Wayback Machine presents them on a calendar.

Enter a business's address at `web.archive.org`, pick a year, and open a capture. Then open the live site alongside it. You are now looking at the same business a decade apart, which is a far better input to a redesign decision than a mood board.

Do this for three or four businesses you actually admire — the restaurant that always has a queue, the trade supplier everybody in the valley uses, the practice that has never advertised. The point is not to copy them but to calibrate how much visual change successful businesses actually undertake.

## What the exercise tends to show

Two patterns come up often enough to be worth naming in advance, and noticing them is the value of the exercise.

**Established businesses change less than the industry implies.** A site that reliably tells people what the business does, where it is and how to buy tends to survive, because there is no internal pressure to alter something that is working. The pressure to redesign usually comes from outside the business.

**When they do change, it is functional.** Sites acquire mobile layouts, faster loading, online ordering, accurate hours — changes that responded to how customers were behaving. The typography is rarely the thing that moved.

That second pattern is the useful one, because it distinguishes two things that get bundled together in a proposal. A redesign that adds online booking is a business decision. A redesign that modernizes the aesthetic is a preference, and preferences are allowed, provided they are priced as preferences rather than as necessities.

## When a redesign is genuinely the right call

None of this argues for never changing anything, and there are conditions where a rebuild is straightforwardly correct.

If nobody can edit the site without the person who built it, that is a rebuild. If it is slow on a phone and the cause is structural, that is a rebuild. If the menus cannot be made keyboard-accessible without replacing them, that is a rebuild — and that one usually arrives alongside other reasons. If the business genuinely does something different from what the site describes, the content is wrong and no amount of styling fixes it.

Notice that all four are functional. None of them is "it looks like 2019".

## What to do instead, most of the time

Before commissioning anything, spend the money's worth of attention on the four questions this site keeps returning to: can somebody find your hours, your location, your prices and a way to reach a person, on a phone, in under thirty seconds.

A site that fails those is worth fixing whether or not it is beautiful. A site that passes them is doing its job, and a redesign is then a discretionary purchase, which is a completely reasonable thing to make as long as everybody involved knows that is what it is.

## The archive has a second use

While you are there, look up your own site.

You will find versions of your own copy you had forgotten, claims you no longer make, prices you no longer charge, and quite possibly a page you thought was deleted. That is worth knowing for its own sake, and it occasionally answers a question about what you told a customer three years ago rather better than memory does.
