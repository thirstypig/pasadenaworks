> **TL;DR** — Nobody can quote a defensible monthly figure without knowing your market, your competition and your starting position, so a proposal that arrives with a price and no diagnosis is selling a package rather than a service. What you can evaluate is the structure: whether the work described is documented as necessary by the platform, whether you would own the results if you left, and whether the proposal states what it will not do. A small local business that is starting from nothing usually needs a few months of focused work rather than an indefinite retainer.

Asking what SEO should cost produces the same unsatisfying answer as asking what a car should cost, and for the same reason. The useful version of the question is narrower: what should *this* work cost, given what my business actually needs, and how would I tell whether I was overpaying?

## Why the honest answer starts with a diagnosis

A proposal that opens with a monthly figure has skipped the step that determines whether the figure means anything.

Two businesses in the same trade, a mile apart, can need entirely different work, and one has a complete Google Business Profile, forty reviews and a site that loads quickly, and needs content and a few links. The other has a duplicate listing nobody claimed, a site built in 2016 that nobody can edit, and an address that is wrong in six directories, and needs several weeks of unglamorous repair before anything else is worth doing.

Any vendor quoting both of those businesses the same number is pricing a package rather than the work. That is not automatically dishonest — packages are how a small agency stays solvent — and you should know that is what you are buying, because a package priced for the second business is poor value for the first.

## Test one: is the work documented as necessary?

This is the sharpest available test, and it costs one question per line item.

When a proposal lists something as required, ask where the platform that operates the thing says it is required. Google's documentation on its AI features is a useful worked example, since it states that there are "no additional requirements to appear in AI Overviews or AI Mode, nor other special optimizations necessary" and that "you don't need to create new machine readable files, AI text files, or markup to appear in these features."

A line item selling AI-specific files as necessary is therefore contradicted by the vendor's own published position. Apply the same test to structured data, to "technical SEO audits" that produce a report and no changes, and to anything described as a requirement rather than as a judgment.

## Test two: do you keep it if you leave?

Work divides into things that stay with you and things that stop the moment you stop paying, and the ratio matters more than the price.

Content on your own site, a corrected Business Profile, fixed directory listings, improved page speed and a site somebody can edit are all assets you keep. Monthly reporting, "ongoing optimization" with no named deliverable, and rented links are not, and A proposal weighted toward the first kind can be expensive and still be good value; one weighted toward the second is a subscription to a service that leaves nothing behind.

Ask the question directly — if we stopped in six months, what would we still have? — because a vendor who answers it well is describing a real engagement rather than a subscription.

## Test three: does it say what it will not do?

A proposal that promises everything has not thought about your business, and the absence of any stated limit is itself information.

Serious proposals contain sentences like "we are not going to compete for the county-wide term, because a single-location business will not win it," or "this will not move for three months." Those sentences cost the vendor the sale sometimes, which is exactly why they are worth hearing.

Be particularly careful with any guarantee of a specific ranking position, and nobody controls a ranking, and a guarantee is either meaningless in the small print or attached to a term so obscure that ranking first for it is worth nothing.

## What a small local business usually needs

For most single-location businesses in the San Gabriel Valley, the honest shape is a concentrated block of work followed by something much smaller.

The concentrated block fixes the profile, corrects the listings, repairs the site's obvious problems, and writes the handful of pages that answer what customers actually ask. That is finite work with a recognizable end state, rather than an open-ended commitment, and what follows is genuinely lighter: reviews, occasional content, keeping details current when something changes.

An indefinite retainer at a constant monthly figure describes neither of those phases accurately, which is worth noticing when one is proposed.

## The number, since you asked

There is no correct figure and the range is enormous, which is a real answer rather than an evasion.

What is more useful: work out what a new customer is worth to you over a year, then ask how many additional customers a month this work would need to produce to pay for itself. If the answer is one or two, the proposal is probably worth trying, and if it is eight, ask the vendor to explain how eight will happen, and read the answer carefully.
