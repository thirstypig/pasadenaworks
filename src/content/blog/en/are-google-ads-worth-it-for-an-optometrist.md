> **TL;DR** — Optometry is one of the better healthcare categories for paid search, because the searches are commercial, local and immediate. It only works if two conditions hold: you can attribute booked appointments back to the ads, and you know what a patient is actually worth once eyewear is included. Without both, you are buying clicks and guessing. Note also that Google applies specific policies to healthcare advertising, and that eye care sits closer to retail than most medical categories do.

Paid search for medical practices is usually a poor fit, because somebody searching a symptom is researching rather than buying and the path from click to appointment is long. Optometry is the exception worth examining, and it is worth understanding why, since the reasons also tell you when to stop.

## Why this category is different

Three properties make optometry unusually suited to paid search, and a practice missing any of them should be more sceptical.

The intent behind the search is commercial rather than investigative. "Eye exam near me", "optometrist open Saturday" and searches for a specific frame brand are people intending to transact, not people reading about a condition. That is a materially different click from the ones a dermatologist would buy.

The purchase decision is made almost immediately. An eye exam is booked within days rather than deliberated for months, so the connection between spending money on Monday and appointments appearing on Thursday is legible in a way it rarely is elsewhere in healthcare.

And the economics of the transaction extend well past the examination itself. An eye exam alone is frequently a modest amount of revenue, and a patient who buys frames and lenses is a substantially larger one. This is the single most important number in the decision and most practices have never calculated it.

## Work out the number first

Before spending anything, establish what a new patient is worth to you across their first year, including eyewear, contact lens supply and any second family member who follows them in.

Then perform the division honestly rather than optimiztically. If a new patient is worth several hundred dollars in the first year and a booked appointment costs you a fraction of that in advertising, the arithmetic works with room to spare. If your practice refers most eyewear out, or sits in a market where patients take the prescription elsewhere, the same click costs the same and returns considerably less, and the answer may simply be no.

This calculation is neither optional nor a formality that can be completed afterwards to justify a decision already taken. It constitutes the entire decision, and every other question raised in this article sits downstream of whatever answer it produces.

## Attribution, or you are guessing

The failure mode that wastes the most money is spending for months without being able to say which appointments the spending produced.

Configure call tracking, because in this category most conversions are telephone calls rather than form submissions, and an untracked call is invisible to the system deciding how to spend your money. Connect the booking system directly to the advertising account if it supports such an integration. And ask at the front desk, every time, recording the answer somewhere consistent — imperfect, and still better than the alternative of pure inference.

Without this, you cannot distinguish an expensive campaign that works from a cheap one that does not, and you will end up making the decision on how the dashboard feels.

## What the policies constrain

Healthcare advertising carries policy conditions that ordinary retail advertising does not, and Google publishes those conditions in documentation anybody can consult beforehand.

Read the healthcare and medicines policy before writing ad copy rather than after a disapproval, since the restrictions affect what may be claimed and, for some categories, whether certification is required at all. Routine optometry sits comfortably within what is permitted, and the practical effect for most practices is on wording rather than eligibility. It is still considerably cheaper to read the policy for twenty minutes than to have a campaign rejected mid-launch.

## Where the money is usually wasted

Three recurring mistakes account for the overwhelming majority of wasted expenditure, and all three of them are entirely avoidable within the first week of a campaign.

Bidding on broad terms such as "eye doctor" without tight geographic targeting buys clicks from people who will never drive to you, and distance is the one thing an ad cannot fix. Sending all traffic to the homepage rather than to a page about eye exams, with the hours and a booking link, loses people who arrived with a specific question. And running ads while the Google Business Profile is incomplete is paying for attention that the free listing would have earned anyway.

Complete the profile first and advertise afterwards, since that ordering costs nothing whatsoever and materially changes what the advertising is subsequently required to accomplish.

## The honest answer

For an independent optometrist who dispenses eyewear, tracks calls, and targets a realistic radius, paid search is one of the few healthcare categories where a small monthly budget can be justified with actual numbers.

For a practice that refers eyewear out, cannot attribute bookings, or is competing with a chain in the same shopping center on brand terms, the money is better spent on the profile, the reviews and the website answering what patients actually ask. That is a less satisfying recommendation than a campaign, and it is more often the correct one.
