> **TL;DR** — What people call "my website" is at least four separate things: the domain name, the hosting account, the files and content, and the Google Business Profile that sends you most of your customers. Each can be registered to a different party, and the person who built the site frequently holds two or three of them. None of this is sinister by default, and all of it is worth establishing while everybody is still on speaking terms.

Almost nobody asks this question while things are going well, which is precisely why it becomes expensive. The question surfaces when a relationship ends, when somebody stops answering, or when a business wants to move to a new supplier and discovers that moving is not entirely theirs to decide.

The reassuring part is that the answer is entirely knowable within an afternoon, and establishing it costs nothing beyond the time spent looking.

## Four things, not one

The mental model responsible for most of the difficulty treats a website as a single indivisible possession, comparable to a delivery vehicle. In practice it is a bundle of separate arrangements that happen to point at each other.

**The domain name** is the address, and it is registered to somebody at a registrar. Whoever is listed as the registrant is the party with authority over it, which includes the authority to move it elsewhere or to let it lapse. This is the single most important item on the list, because a business that loses its domain loses its email, its search history, and every link anybody ever made to it.

**The hosting account** is the machine the files physically reside on, and it is billed to somebody who can consequently alter what is served, or discontinue payment entirely, quite independently of whoever controls the domain.

**The files and the content** — the design, the code, the photographs, the words — are their own question, governed by whatever your contract says and, in its absence, by rather less than most people assume.

**The Google Business Profile** is the fourth, and for a business with a physical location it is frequently the most valuable of the four, because it is where the map listing, the reviews and the telephone number live, and it maintains its own separate notion of who owns it, independent of everything else on this list.

A supplier holding all four is not necessarily behaving badly, since it is very often simply how things were set up on a Tuesday afternoon three years ago by somebody being helpful and moving quickly.

## Why the domain matters more than the rest

If you only establish one thing, establish this one, because the others are recoverable in ways the domain is not.

A site can be rebuilt, hosting can be replaced in an afternoon, and content can be rewritten — as, frequently, it should be anyway. But a domain that somebody else controls is a domain that can be moved, sold, allowed to expire, or simply held while a negotiation happens — and the business on the other end of it has very little leverage while that is true.

Domain transfers between registrars are governed by ICANN's transfer policy, which is public and which also provides a complaint route when a transfer is refused improperly — a genuine protection, and also a conspicuously slow one. It is a much better position to be the registrant in the first place than to be correct in a dispute about it.

## How to find out, this week

Start with the domain, since it is the item that matters most and the one you can check without anybody's cooperation. A public WHOIS or ICANN lookup will tell you which registrar holds it and when it expires. Privacy services will often mask the registrant's name, which is normal and not itself a warning sign, but you should still be able to establish which registrar the domain lives at — and if you cannot log in to an account there, you do not currently control it.

Then check the Google Business Profile, which has its own ownership independent of everything else. Google documents a process for requesting ownership from whoever currently holds a profile: the current owner is notified by email and has three days to respond, and if they do not, you may have the option to claim it, though Google notes that option is not always available. Three days is a short window, and it is a great deal shorter than the argument you would otherwise be having.

Finally, ask your supplier plainly which accounts are in their name and which are in yours. A good one will answer immediately and may well offer to move things across, and the quality of that answer is itself worth knowing regardless of what it turns out to be.

## What a reasonable arrangement looks like

None of this argues that you ought to administer everything yourself, since most business owners neither want to manage a registrar account nor have any particular reason to.

The arrangement that genuinely protects a business is not performing the technical work itself, but retaining ownership of the underlying accounts. Register the domain in the business's name, on a card the business controls, and grant your supplier access to it. Own the hosting account while adding them as a user, and be the owner of the Google Business Profile while making them a manager on it. Under that arrangement they continue doing the work while you hold the keys, which means either party can walk away from the relationship without the departure itself becoming an incident.

Any supplier who resists that arrangement is telling you something useful about how the relationship ends. It is a reasonable thing to ask before you sign, it is an awkward thing to ask afterwards, and it is a genuinely difficult thing to ask once you have already fallen out.
